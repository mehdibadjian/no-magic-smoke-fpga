# Section 4.2: Placement and Routing: The Digital Tetris

If synthesis is the forge where your logic is created, then **Placement and Routing (P&R)** is the game of Digital Tetris where those pieces are fitted onto the physical silicon. In an AMD FPGA, you have millions of tiny "slots" (LUTs, registers, and interconnects). Your logic blocks need to be placed in these slots and then connected by a complex web of tiny copper wires.

This isn't just about making things fit; it's about making them fit **perfectly**. If two blocks that talk to each other are placed on opposite sides of the chip, the wire connecting them will be long and slow. In the high-speed world of FPGA design, every millimeter of wire adds nanoseconds of delay.

### Digital Tetris: The Physical Grid

The placement engine’s job is to group related logic together. It looks at your design and tries to keep "clusters" of logic in the same neighborhood. This is why a clean hierarchy (from Section 3.2) is so important—it gives the tool a hint about which blocks should stay together.

But sometimes, the "Tetris pieces" don't fit easily. If you have a design that is 95% full, the tool has almost no room to move things around. This leads to "Sub-optimal Placement," where the tool is forced to put critical components far apart just to make them fit. **Aim for 70-80% utilization** for a comfortable design; anything higher is playing Tetris on "Expert" mode.

> **Why does a design with 99% utilization often fail timing, even if the logic itself is very simple? (Hint: Think about the routing 'channels' between the logic blocks.)**

### Congestion: The Traffic Jam from Hell

**Congestion** is the number one enemy of routing. It happens when too many wires are trying to pass through the same narrow area of the chip. Imagine a ten-lane highway that suddenly narrows down to a single-lane bridge. That bridge is a "Congestion Hotspot."

When the routing engine hits a hotspot, it has to "detour" wires around the area. These detours make the wires longer, which increases delay and kills your timing. To avoid congestion, keep your logic modular and avoid "Global" signals that have to touch every single block. If you see congestion in your reports, you might need to spread your logic out manually.

### Fireside Chat: The Floorplanner and the Auto-Router

**Floorplanner:** "I’ve spent all morning creating PBLOCKs for the DSP engine. I’ve locked it down to the bottom-left corner of the chip to keep it away from the noisy PCIe interface."

**Auto-Router:** "That’s cute. But by locking that logic down, you’ve created a massive bottleneck at the edge of your PBLOCK. I’m trying to route 512 wires out of that corner, and there just isn't enough copper."

**Floorplanner:** "But if I don't lock it down, you'll scatter the logic all over the die and I’ll never meet my 400MHz target!"

**Auto-Router:** "I’m not a child. If you give me a 'Soft Constraint' instead of a 'Hard PBLOCK,' I can move things around just enough to clear the congestion while still keeping the logic relatively close together. Give me some breathing room, and I’ll give you a design that meets timing."

### SLR Crossings: The Great Divide

In larger AMD FPGAs (the "UltraScale+" family), the chip is actually made of multiple separate silicon dies called **Super Logic Regions (SLRs)**. These dies are connected by a special high-speed bridge called a **Super Long Line (SLL)**.

Crossing an SLR boundary is a big deal. It adds a significant amount of delay—equivalent to several nanoseconds. If you have a critical path that crosses an SLR, you **must** pipeline it. This means putting a register on both sides of the boundary to "catch" the data and give the tools a full clock cycle to make the jump. Ignore this, and the "Great Divide" will swallow your timing whole.

### PBLOCKS: The Zoning Laws of Silicon

Sometimes the auto-placement engine needs a little help. **PBLOCKs (Physical Blocks)** are manual constraints where you tell Vivado, "This specific module must stay within this specific rectangular area of the silicon."

Think of PBLOCKs as zoning laws. You’re telling the tool where the "Industrial Zone" (the high-speed math) and the "Residential Zone" (the slow control logic) should be. PBLOCKs are powerful, but they are also dangerous. If you make a PBLOCK too small, you create congestion. If you make it too big, you waste area. Use them as a last resort when the auto-tools are failing.

### Code Detective: The Unbuffered Clock

A common routing disaster is an "Unbuffered Clock." In an FPGA, clocks must use dedicated "Global Clock" lines.

```tcl
# Code Detective: Why will this lead to a routing failure?
set_property PACKAGE_PIN A10 [get_ports my_clock]
# No BUFG or IBUFG instantiated in the HDL...
```

The flaw here is that the clock signal is being treated like a regular data signal. Instead of using the dedicated, low-skew high-speed clock network, the tool will try to route the clock through the regular "fabric" wires. This creates massive **Clock Skew**, where different parts of your design see the clock edge at different times.

In practice Vivado will usually stop you rather than let you ship it, and the error it
produces is one worth recognising on sight:

```text
ERROR: [Place 30-574] Poor placement for routing between an IO pin and BUFG.
  If this sub optimal condition is acceptable for this design, you may use the
  CLOCK_DEDICATED_ROUTE constraint in the .xdc file to demote this message to
  a WARNING...
```

That message means the clock-capable pin you chose is not in a clock region that can reach
the `BUFGCE` the placer wants to use. **The temptation is to paste the constraint the
error message suggests. Do not.** Demoting the message tells the tool to route your clock
through fabric anyway — you have silenced the alarm, not fixed the fault.

**The fix**, in order of preference:

```systemverilog
// 1. Best: bring the clock in on a clock-capable (CC) pin and buffer it explicitly.
IBUFDS u_ibuf (.I(sys_clk_p), .IB(sys_clk_n), .O(clk_ibuf));
BUFGCE u_bufg (.I(clk_ibuf), .CE(1'b1), .O(clk));
```

```tcl
# 2. Confirm the pin you chose is actually clock-capable, BEFORE the board is built:
#    IS_GLOBAL_CLK is the property that matters.
get_property IS_GLOBAL_CLK [get_package_pins A10]

# 3. Only if the board is already fabricated and the pin is wrong is the constraint
#    justified - and then it is a documented compromise, not a fix:
#    set_property CLOCK_DEDICATED_ROUTE FALSE [get_nets my_clock]
#    Record the measured skew in the design documentation so the next person knows
#    the timing report is optimistic.

# 4. Verify what you actually got:
report_clock_networks -file clk_networks.rpt
#    Every clock should show a BUFG-class driver. A clock whose driver is a LUT or
#    an IBUF is being routed through fabric.
```

The general principle: **an error message that offers you a constraint to silence it is
describing a trade, not a solution.** Read what you would be trading away before you
accept.

### Analogy → Silicon

| In the story | In the silicon | Where the analogy breaks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Digital Tetris | `place_design` assigning cells to CLB/DSP/BRAM sites | Tetris pieces are rigid and the board is empty at the start; placement is a continuous optimisation over a partly-constrained device, and "fits" is not the goal — "fits with slack" is |
| Ten lanes narrowing to one | **Routing congestion**: demand for interconnect exceeding supply in a region | Traffic redistributes over time; routing demand is fixed by your netlist, so a congested region does not clear — it fails, or it detours and costs you timing |
| Zoning laws | **Pblocks** (`create_pblock`, `resize_pblock`) | Zoning constrains what may be built; a pblock constrains *where*, and an over-tight one manufactures the congestion it was meant to prevent |
| The great divide | SLR boundaries crossed via SLLs and Laguna registers | A divide is a line on a map; this one has a fixed resource budget and a delay you must budget a whole cycle for (Section 1.4) |
| Soft constraint vs hard PBLOCK | `CONTAIN_ROUTING` / `EXCLUDE_PLACEMENT` off vs on | "Soft" sounds like a weaker version of the same thing; it is a different thing — it lets the placer relieve pressure at the edges, which is usually what makes the pblock work at all |

### Do the Math

#### Utilisation versus routability: why 99 % fails and 75 % does not

Each CLB has a finite number of routing tracks entering and leaving it. Placement fills
*logic* sites; routing consumes *interconnect*, and the two run out at different points.

**Givens** — a simplified model that captures the effect:

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Logic sites in a region | 1,000 CLBs |
| Routing tracks available per region | 24,000 track-segments |
| Average track-segments consumed per placed CLB | 20 |

At 75 % logic utilisation:

$$750\ \text{CLBs} \times 20 = 15{,}000\ \text{track-segments} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \frac{15{,}000}{24{,}000} = 63\ \%\ \text{routing demand}$$

At 99 %:

$$990 \times 20 = 19{,}800 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \frac{19{,}800}{24{,}000} = 83\ \%$$

83 % does not sound fatal — but routing demand is not uniform, and this is the part the
average hides. **The router must detour whenever a local hotspot exceeds 100 %**, and each
detour lengthens a net, which consumes yet more track-segments, which pushes neighbouring
regions toward their own limit. The failure is *super-linear*: at 63 % average demand a
hotspot at 1.5× the average is still routable; at 83 % the same hotspot is at 125 % and
must detour, and its detours push its neighbours over too.

That is why the practical guidance is a utilisation *band* rather than a limit:

| Logic utilisation | What to expect |
| --- | --- |
| < 70 % | Placement and routing are not your problem. Look elsewhere. |
| 70–85 % | Normal. Congestion is manageable with directives and modest floorplanning. |
| 85–90 % | Every run gets slower, results get noisier, incremental changes have non-local effects. |
| > 90 % | You are buying timing closure with engineering weeks. Consider a bigger device — it is usually cheaper than the schedule. |

#### Reading congestion levels

`report_design_analysis -congestion` reports congestion on a **1–8 scale** per region, for
both placement and routing. The scale is logarithmic in the window size the router had to
search:

| Level | Meaning | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1–2 | Nothing to see | — |
| 3–4 | Mild, localised | Usually resolves itself; note it and move on |
| 5–6 | Real congestion | Act: reduce fanout, spread logic, revisit a pblock |
| 7–8 | Severe | Routing will likely fail or timing will collapse. Architectural fix needed. |

**Level 5 is the threshold that matters.** Below it, spend your time on timing; at or above
it, congestion *is* your timing problem and fixing paths individually will not help.

### The Real Artifact

Congestion diagnosis, then a pblock that helps rather than hurts. Both are scripts you
keep, not steps you remember.

```tcl
#==============================================================================
# congestion_triage.tcl - run after place_design, before you change anything.
#
# The mistake this script exists to prevent: reacting to a congested design by
# tightening pblocks. Tight pblocks CAUSE congestion. Diagnose first.
#==============================================================================

proc congestion_triage {} {
    puts "\n=== 1. Where is it congested, and how badly? ==="
    # Levels are 1-8 per region for placement and routing. >= 5 needs action.
    report_design_analysis -congestion -name congestion

    puts "\n=== 2. Is it complexity (Rent exponent) or just density? ==="
    # A high Rent exponent means the netlist itself is highly interconnected -
    # that is an RTL problem, and no placement directive will fix it.
    report_design_analysis -complexity -name complexity

    puts "\n=== 3. High-fanout nets are the most common single cause ==="
    report_high_fanout_nets -timing -load_types -max_nets 25

    puts "\n=== 4. Did any pblock become the bottleneck? ==="
    foreach pb [get_pblocks -quiet] {
        set cells [get_cells -quiet -of_objects $pb]
        set stats [report_utilization -pblocks $pb -return_string]
        puts "  pblock [get_property NAME $pb]: [llength $cells] cell(s)"
        puts "    (check the CLB row of this report - over ~80% inside a pblock is"
        puts "     where a floorplan starts working against you)"
    }

    puts "\n=== 5. What does Vivado suggest? ==="
    report_qor_suggestions -max_paths 10
}

congestion_triage
```

```tcl
#==============================================================================
# floorplan.xdc - a pblock that helps.
#
# Four rules, learned expensively:
#   1. Size it from measured utilisation, not from intuition. Target <= 70%
#      utilisation INSIDE the pblock, because the placer needs slack to route.
#   2. Leave CONTAIN_ROUTING false unless you have proven you need it. Containing
#      routing inside a tight boundary is the fastest way to manufacture congestion.
#   3. Align to CLOCKREGION boundaries. A pblock that cuts a clock region in half
#      splits the clock tree and buys you skew you did not want.
#   4. Constrain the module that has a REASON to be somewhere - next to its I/O,
#      next to its memory controller, inside one SLR. Do not pblock logic just
#      because it is large.
#==============================================================================

# The transceiver-facing datapath must live near its GT sites. That is the reason.
create_pblock pblk_gt_datapath
add_cells_to_pblock pblk_gt_datapath [get_cells -hier -filter {NAME =~ "*u_gt_rx_path*"}]
resize_pblock pblk_gt_datapath -add CLOCKREGION_X0Y2:CLOCKREGION_X1Y5

# Soft by default: the placer may spill at the edges to relieve pressure.
set_property CONTAIN_ROUTING    false [get_pblocks pblk_gt_datapath]
set_property EXCLUDE_PLACEMENT  false [get_pblocks pblk_gt_datapath]

# Check the size is right, every run:
#   report_utilization -pblocks [get_pblocks pblk_gt_datapath]
# If CLB utilisation inside the pblock exceeds ~70%, GROW IT. A pblock at 90%
# is a congestion generator, and it will make the design worse than no pblock.

#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Fanout control - cheaper and more effective than floorplanning, and the first
# thing to try when report_high_fanout_nets names a culprit.
#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Replicate the driver of a high-fanout control net so each copy serves a
# local group. 'MAX_FANOUT' asks synthesis to do it for you:
set_property MAX_FANOUT 200 [get_nets u_ctrl/global_enable]

# For resets specifically, prefer a proper reset tree in RTL over MAX_FANOUT -
# a replicated reset whose copies deassert in different cycles is a new bug.

#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Implementation directives that specifically target congestion. Try in this
# order; each is one implementation run.
#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
#   place_design    -directive AltSpreadLogic_high
#   place_design    -directive SSI_SpreadLogic_high     (SSI devices)
#   phys_opt_design -directive AggressiveExplore
#   route_design    -directive AlternateCLBRouting
```

### Final Thoughts on the Grid

Placement and Routing is where the "rubber meets the road." It’s the final test of your design's physical viability. By respecting congestion, pipelining SLR crossings, and using PBLOCKs wisely, you turn the "Digital Tetris" into a winning game. Keep your logic modular, give the tools some room to breathe, and watch your design slide perfectly into place.

### Answers

**Brain Power** — *Why does a 99 % utilised design fail timing even when the logic is
simple?*

Because **logic sites and routing resources run out at different rates**, and timing is
paid for out of the second one.

At 99 % logic utilisation the placer has essentially no freedom. Two cells that want to be
adjacent get placed wherever there is a hole, which may be a long way apart. Then the
router, which has almost no spare track-segments in that region, cannot take the direct
path either — so it detours. Each detour lengthens a net, and a longer net both costs
delay directly *and* consumes more track-segments, raising demand for every other net in
the region. The effect is super-linear, which is why 90 % feels manageable and 99 % feels
like a wall.

Concretely, the same simple logic that routes in 200 ps at 70 % utilisation may route in
900 ps at 99 %, and there is no RTL change that addresses it — the netlist was never the
problem.

There is a second, subtler mechanism worth knowing: at extreme utilisation the placer
starts making *packing* compromises, putting flops with different control sets into
suboptimal arrangements and spreading what should be a tight cluster across several CLBs.
So even the "simple logic" gets physically larger than it needed to be.

The practical conclusion: **utilisation is a timing parameter, not just a capacity
parameter.** If you are above 90 % and fighting timing, price a bigger device against the
engineering weeks. The device is usually cheaper.

**Sharpen Your Pencil #1** — Level 6 congestion at 68 % overall utilisation.

The combination is the diagnostic. Overall utilisation is *comfortable*, so the design as
a whole is not too big for the device — which means the congestion is **local**, not
global. Something is concentrating interconnect demand in one region.

What to check, in order:

1. **`report_high_fanout_nets -timing -load_types`.** A single net with thousands of loads
   whose driver sits in that region is the most common cause, and the cheapest to fix
   (`MAX_FANOUT`, or replication in RTL).
2. **`report_design_analysis -complexity`** for that region. A high Rent exponent means
   the *netlist* in that region is intrinsically highly interconnected — a crossbar, a
   large multiplexer, a wide shared bus. That is an RTL problem, and placement directives
   will not fix it.
3. **Pblocks.** If you have one covering or bordering that region, check its internal
   utilisation. A pblock at 90 % inside is a congestion generator even when the device is
   at 68 %.
4. **Hard block placement.** A memory controller, transceiver bank or HBM interface at one
   edge pulls all its traffic through the fabric next to it. That congestion is
   structural, and the fix is floorplanning the *consumers* nearer to it.

What *not* to do: reach for a placement directive first. Directives redistribute
congestion; they do not remove demand. Find what is generating the demand.

**Sharpen Your Pencil #2** —

$$\frac{4{,}000}{4{,}200} = 95\ \%\ \text{utilisation inside the pblock}$$

**It will hurt, badly.** You have created a region at 95 % utilisation inside a device that
may be comfortable everywhere else — precisely the condition from the Brain Power answer,
except you built it deliberately. The placer has no room to arrange logic well inside the
boundary, the router has no spare tracks, and every net that must leave the pblock has to
squeeze through an edge that is also saturated.

The correct size, targeting ≤ 70 % inside:

$$\text{Required} = \frac{4{,}000}{0.70} \approx 5{,}715\ \text{CLBs}$$

So roughly 5,700–6,000 CLBs, aligned outward to clock region boundaries. And check it
every run:

```tcl
report_utilization -pblocks [get_pblocks pblk_name]
```

The general lesson: **a pblock is a hint about locality, not a container you fill.** If you
find yourself sizing one tightly to "not waste area," you have misunderstood what it is
for — the area inside a pblock is not consumed by the pblock, it is available to whatever
the placer wants to put there, and leaving room is the entire point.

**Sharpen Your Pencil #3** — `[Place 30-574]`, board already fabricated, clock on a
non-clock-capable pin.

What you will do:

1. **Verify the pin really is not clock-capable**, rather than the placer simply having
   chosen a distant `BUFGCE`:

    ```tcl
    get_property IS_GLOBAL_CLK [get_package_pins <pin>]
    ```

    If it *is* clock-capable, the fix is placement — constrain the `BUFGCE` to a clock
    region the pin can reach, and the error goes away legitimately.

2. **If it genuinely is not**, accept the compromise explicitly:

    ```tcl
    set_property CLOCK_DEDICATED_ROUTE FALSE [get_nets my_clock]
    ```

    and immediately bound the damage: get the clock onto a `BUFGCE` as close to the pin as
    possible so only the pin-to-buffer segment is on fabric routing, not the whole tree.

3. **Measure the consequence.** Run `report_clock_networks` and, more importantly, look at
   the clock skew reported on paths in that domain. Fabric routing on the input segment
   adds delay and, more dangerously, adds *variation* — which shows up as increased
   uncertainty on every path in that domain.

4. **Consider derating the interface.** If this clock feeds a source-synchronous input,
   the extra insertion delay changes your input delay budget (Section 2.2). Recompute it.

What you will document, and this is the part that matters:

- That `CLOCK_DEDICATED_ROUTE FALSE` is present, on which net, and **why** — "board rev A
  routed the clock to pin X which is not clock-capable."
- The measured skew and the timing margin lost.
- **A board change request for the next revision.** The constraint is a workaround for a
  hardware defect, and if nobody records it as such, revision B will have the same pin
  assignment and the next engineer will find a mysterious constraint with no explanation.

That last bullet is the difference between a compromise and a landmine.

### There Are No Dumb Questions

**Q: Should I floorplan every design?**

A: No. Under about 70 % utilisation, on a single-SLR device, with no hard-block affinity
to respect, the placer will beat your intuition and a pblock will only get in its way.
Floorplan when there is a *reason* — an SLR boundary to respect, a hard block to sit next
to, a repeating structure to align, or a proven congestion hotspot. "The design is big" is
not a reason.

**Q: `phys_opt_design` did nothing. Is it broken?**

A: It has a narrow remit: it makes *physical* optimisations to paths that are already
close — replicating drivers, retiming across placed registers, rewiring equivalent
outputs. If your worst path is failing by 800 ps with 11 levels of logic, there is nothing
physical to optimise and it will correctly report no improvement. Its sweet spot is the
last 50–150 ps. Try `-directive AggressiveExplore` when you are in that range, and
something else when you are not.

**Q: What is the Rent exponent in `report_design_analysis -complexity`, in practical
terms?**

A: A measure of how interconnected a region's netlist is — how many external connections
a block of logic needs, as a function of its size. A high value means the logic is
intrinsically hard to partition, so no placement can make its wires short. Practically: a
high Rent exponent tells you the congestion is coming from your *architecture* (a
crossbar, a full mesh, a wide shared bus) and that placement tuning will not help. It is
the number that tells you to stop tuning and go change the RTL.

**Q: Can I just let the router run longer?**

A: Routing effort is controlled by directives rather than by time, and `-directive
Explore` or `AggressiveExplore` genuinely do search harder. But if the router is failing
because demand exceeds supply in a region, more effort finds more detours, not a solution
— and detours cost timing. A router that runs for six hours and succeeds has usually
produced a design whose timing is worse than one that routed in forty minutes.

**Q: How do I stop the placer scattering my module across the device?**

A: First check that it is actually scattered rather than that you assume it is —
`highlight_objects [get_cells -hier -filter {NAME =~ "*u_mod*"}]` in the Device view
answers it in seconds. If it is, the cause is usually that something in the module is
tied to a distant resource (an I/O, a hard block, a clock), and the placer is trading
module locality for that connection. Fix the cause, or pblock the module — generously
sized — so the trade is made your way.

### Bullet Points

- **Utilisation is a timing parameter.** < 70 %: not your problem. 70–85 %: normal.
  85–90 %: everything gets slower and noisier. > 90 %: you are buying closure with
  engineering weeks.
- **Congestion levels run 1–8. Level 5 is the threshold for action.** Below it, work on
  timing; at or above it, congestion *is* your timing problem.
- **Local congestion at low overall utilisation means a local cause**: high fanout, a
  hard-block hotspot, a high Rent-exponent structure, or a too-tight pblock.
- **Size pblocks for ≤ 70 % internal utilisation.** A pblock at 95 % manufactures the
  congestion it was meant to prevent. Room inside is not waste.
- **Leave `CONTAIN_ROUTING` false** unless you have proven you need it, and align pblocks
  to `CLOCKREGION` boundaries.
- **Fanout before floorplan.** `report_high_fanout_nets` then `MAX_FANOUT` or RTL
  replication is cheaper and more effective than a pblock.
- **`[Place 30-574]` is a hardware finding, not a constraint request.** Fix the pin if you
  can; if you cannot, apply `CLOCK_DEDICATED_ROUTE FALSE`, measure the skew, and file a
  board change.
- **Congestion directives**: `place_design -directive AltSpreadLogic_high` /
  `SSI_SpreadLogic_high`, `phys_opt_design -directive AggressiveExplore`,
  `route_design -directive AlternateCLBRouting`.
- **Reports**: `report_design_analysis -congestion -complexity`,
  `report_high_fanout_nets -timing -load_types`, `report_utilization -pblocks`,
  `report_clock_networks`.
