# Section 5.1: Power Estimation and Optimization: The Energy Budget

In the early days of FPGAs, power was an afterthought. But as chips have gotten smaller and faster, they’ve also gotten hungrier. Today, **Power Management** is a first-class citizen in the AMD UltraFast Methodology. If your FPGA consumes too much power, it will get too hot, its performance will degrade, and it might even melt the solder off your board.

Think of your FPGA as a high-performance sports car. If you drive it at 200mph all day, you're going to burn a lot of fuel and generate a lot of heat. Power estimation is your "Fuel Gauge," and optimization is your "Fuel Efficiency" package.

### The Energy Budget: Static vs. Dynamic Power

Power consumption in an FPGA comes in two flavors:

1.  **Static Power (Leakage):** This is the power the chip consumes just by being turned on. Even if no logic is switching, electrons are "leaking" through the transistors. Static power is mostly a function of the chip’s size and temperature.
2.  **Dynamic Power:** This is the power consumed when your logic signals toggle from 0 to 1. Every time a wire changes state, it charges and discharges tiny capacitors, which consumes energy. Dynamic power is a function of your clock frequency and how much logic is active.

Managing your energy budget means attacking both types of power. You can't do much about static power (other than keeping the chip cool), but you have total control over dynamic power.

> **Brain Power**
> **If you double the clock frequency of your design, does the dynamic power consumption double, quadruple, or stay the same? Why does reducing the voltage have a much larger impact on power than reducing the frequency?**

### XPE: The Financial Planner for Power

AMD provides a powerful tool called the **Xilinx Power Estimator (XPE)**. It’s a sophisticated spreadsheet that allows you to predict your power consumption before you even write a single line of code.

Think of XPE as your "Financial Planner." You tell it which FPGA you’re using, your expected clock frequencies, and how many LUTs and DSPs you plan to use. XPE then gives you a detailed breakdown of your power needs. This allows you to design your Power Delivery Network (PDN) and your cooling system *before* the hardware is built. Don't build a board without an XPE report; it’s like going on a shopping spree without checking your bank balance.

### Fireside Chat: The Performance Junkie and the Green Engineer

**Performance Junkie:** "I’ve got the DSP engine running at 600MHz! The data throughput is incredible! We’re going to crush the competition."

**Green Engineer:** "That’s great, but the chip is pulling 50 Watts and the heatsink is glowing red. We’re going to kill the battery in twenty minutes, and the fan is so loud it sounds like a jet engine."

**Performance Junkie:** "So what? Speed is everything!"

**Green Engineer:** "Not if the chip shuts down due to over-temperature. I can use 'Clock Gating' to turn off the DSP blocks when they aren't processing data. We’ll keep 90% of the performance but cut the power consumption in half. Efficiency is the ultimate performance hack."

### Clock Gating: Turning Off the Lights

The most effective way to save dynamic power is **Clock Gating**. This means disabling the clock signal to parts of your design that aren't currently being used. If the clock doesn't toggle, the flip-flops don't toggle, and the dynamic power drops to zero.

Vivado can perform some clock gating automatically, but for the best results, you should design it into your RTL. Use "Clock Enables" (CE) on your registers. When CE is low, the register stays silent. It’s like turning off the lights in a room when you leave—it’s a simple habit that saves a ton of money (and heat).

### Voltage Scaling: The Lean Diet

AMD FPGAs often support multiple voltage levels for their internal logic. By running your chip at a slightly lower voltage (e.g., 0.72V instead of 0.85V), you can drastically reduce power consumption. This is because power is proportional to the **square** of the voltage ($P \propto V^2$).

However, lower voltage also means slower transistors. You have to balance your desire for low power with your need for high speed. The UltraFast methodology recommends using the "VCCINT_IO" and "VCCINT" scaling features wisely to find the "Sweet Spot" for your specific application.

### Code Detective: The Glitchy Logic Power Drain

Combinational "Glitches" can be a silent power killer. A glitch is when a logic signal toggles multiple times before settling on its final value.

```verilog
// Code Detective: Why will this logic consume unnecessary power?
assign out = (a & b) | (c & d) | (e & f);
// Hint: If a, b, c, d, e, and f all change at slightly different times, how many times will 'out' toggle?
```

The flaw here is a combinational "SOP" (Sum of Products) tree whose inputs arrive at different times. Each arrival can flip the output before it settles, and every one of those extra transitions charges and discharges real capacitance.

Put a number on it, because the effect compounds through a logic cone. If the six inputs
arrive at six different times, the output can transition up to six times per evaluation
instead of once — and each downstream stage sees those glitches as *its* input activity,
multiplying them again. In a deep unregistered arithmetic tree, glitch power routinely
exceeds useful switching power.

**The fix — and the more important second half of it:**

```verilog
// Fix 1: register the output. The flop samples once per clock edge, so the
// glitches never leave this stage. Cost: one cycle of latency, one flop.
always_ff @(posedge clk) begin
    out <= (a & b) | (c & d) | (e & f);
end

// Fix 2 (the one people miss): balance the arrival times. Registering the OUTPUT
// stops glitches propagating onward, but the tree itself still glitches internally,
// and that power is still burned. Registering the INPUTS makes them arrive together,
// which is what actually stops the glitching.
always_ff @(posedge clk) begin
    a_r <= a;  b_r <= b;  c_r <= c;  d_r <= d;  e_r <= e;  f_r <= f;
    out <= (a_r & b_r) | (c_r & d_r) | (e_r & f_r);
end
```

Then measure rather than assume, because glitch power is exactly the component that
estimation tools get wrong without activity data:

```tcl
# report_power's default toggle assumptions cannot see glitching. A SAIF from
# gate-level simulation can, because it records actual transition counts.
read_saif  -strip_path tb_top/dut ./sim/post_route.saif
report_power -file power_saif.rpt

# Check the confidence level at the top of the report. "Low" means the tool is
# guessing at toggle rates and the number below it is not an engineering figure.
```

### Analogy → Silicon

| In the story | In the silicon | Where the analogy breaks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fuel gauge | XPE at planning time; `report_power` on the routed design | A fuel gauge measures; both of these *estimate*, and their accuracy depends entirely on the activity data you feed them — hence the SAIF flow |
| Turning off the lights | **Clock enables** (`CE`) and `BUFGCE` gating | Lights go fully off; a gated flop still leaks, and the clock tree upstream of the gate still toggles — you save the leaf, not the trunk |
| A lean diet | Voltage scaling ($P \propto V^2$) | Dieting is gradual and reversible; lowering $V_{CCINT}$ slows transistors, so it is a *joint* power/timing decision, and it makes your PDN target impedance harder (Section 1.2) |
| Sports car at 200 mph | Dynamic power $\propto \alpha C V^2 f$ | A car burns fuel proportional to speed; dynamic power is proportional to *toggling*, so a fast clock feeding idle logic costs far less than the frequency alone suggests |
| The financial planner | XPE's per-resource breakdown | A planner works from known income; XPE works from *your* estimates of resource counts and toggle rates, so its output is only as good as those guesses |

### Do the Math

#### The one equation, and what each term is worth

$$P_{\text{dynamic}} = \alpha \, C \, V^2 f$$

where $\alpha$ is the activity factor (fraction of clock cycles on which a node toggles),
$C$ the switched capacitance, $V$ the supply voltage and $f$ the clock frequency.

Each term is a different lever with a different exponent, and that is the whole story:

| Lever | Effect on power | Effect on the design |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $f$ ÷ 2 | **linear** — halves dynamic power | Halves throughput. Usually unacceptable. |
| $\alpha$ ÷ 2 (clock enables) | **linear** — halves dynamic power in the gated logic | Costs a control signal. Usually free. |
| $C$ ÷ 2 (less logic, shorter nets) | **linear** | Requires design work. |
| $V$ ÷ 2 | **quadratic** — quarters dynamic power | Transistors get slower; often not available at all. |

**Worked example.** Take a datapath and compute the saving from clock enables.

**Givens:**

| Symbol | Meaning | Value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $P_{\text{dyn}}$ | Dynamic power of the DSP datapath, always-on | 12.0 W |
| Duty | Fraction of cycles the datapath has valid data | 30 % |
| $P_{\text{clock tree}}$ | Portion of that which is the clock network itself | 3.5 W |

Gating the *data* registers with a `CE` reduces $\alpha$ on the logic but leaves the clock
tree toggling:

$$P_{\text{new}} = \underbrace{3.5}_{\text{clock tree, unchanged}} + \underbrace{(12.0 - 3.5) \times 0.30}_{\text{logic, now gated}} = 3.5 + 2.55 = 6.05\ \text{W}$$

$$\text{Saving} = 12.0 - 6.05 = 5.95\ \text{W} \quad (50\ \%)$$

Now gate the **clock** as well, using `BUFGCE` so the tree itself stops:

$$P_{\text{new}} = (3.5 + 8.5) \times 0.30 = 12.0 \times 0.30 = 3.6\ \text{W}$$

$$\text{Saving} = 8.4\ \text{W} \quad (70\ \%)$$

**Verdict: register enables get you half of the available saving; the other half is in the
clock tree, and it needs `BUFGCE`, not `CE`.** That distinction — leaf versus trunk — is
the one most power reviews miss.

#### Why voltage scaling is so tempting and so constrained

$$\frac{P_{0.72}}{P_{0.85}} = \left(\frac{0.72}{0.85}\right)^2 = 0.72$$

A 15 % voltage reduction gives a **28 % dynamic power reduction** — better than any other
single lever. The catch is that transistor delay rises as voltage falls, so your $F_{max}$
drops, and (from Section 1.2) your PDN target impedance:

$$Z_{\text{target}} = \frac{V \times \text{ripple}}{\Delta I}$$

gets *harder* on both terms. Voltage scaling is a system decision made at board design
time, not a knob you turn during closure.

#### Static power, and why it is a thermal problem

Static (leakage) power rises steeply with junction temperature, which raises temperature
further, which raises leakage — a positive feedback loop called thermal runaway in its
extreme form. Practically: your leakage figure at 100 °C can be several times its 25 °C
value, so **you cannot compute a power budget without also computing a thermal solution**,
and vice versa. That is why this section and the next are a pair, and why `report_power`
asks you for a junction temperature.

### The Real Artifact

The power flow, in the order that actually produces trustworthy numbers, plus the RTL
pattern for the clock gating computed above.

```tcl
#==============================================================================
# power_flow.tcl - from a guess to an engineering number.
#
# The single most common mistake in FPGA power work: quoting report_power's
# default output as if it were a measurement. Its default toggle rate is an
# assumption (typically 12.5% on data nets). Feed it real activity or say
# "estimate" out loud.
#==============================================================================

open_checkpoint ./build/post_route.dcp

#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 1. The default estimate. Fast, and its CONFIDENCE LEVEL is the point - check it.
#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
report_power -file ./build/power_default.rpt
# Read the "Confidence Level" table at the top:
#   Low    - the tool is guessing at toggle rates. Do not quote this number.
#   Medium - some activity data, or a well-characterised design.
#   High   - vectorless with good constraints, or SAIF-driven.

#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 2. The real number. Requires a simulation that exercises a representative
#    workload - not a reset test, not a corner case: the thing the product does.
#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
#   In simulation:
#     open_saif ./sim/post_route.saif
#     log_saif  [get_objects -r /tb_top/dut/*]
#     run 100us
#     close_saif
#
read_saif -strip_path tb_top/dut ./sim/post_route.saif
report_power -file ./build/power_saif.rpt

#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Where is it going? The per-hierarchy breakdown tells you where to spend
#    effort, and it is almost never where people assume.
#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
report_power -hierarchical_depth 3 -file ./build/power_hier.rpt

#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 4. What does the tool suggest? This names specific nets and specific fixes.
#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
report_power_opt   -file ./build/power_opt.rpt
power_opt_design                      ;# automatic clock gating insertion

#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 5. Thermal cross-check - power and temperature are one problem (Section 5.2).
#    Set the operating conditions you will actually ship into, not the defaults.
#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
set_operating_conditions -ambient_temp 55.0 -airflow 250 -heatsink medium
report_power -file ./build/power_hot.rpt
# Compare the static power line against power_saif.rpt. If leakage moved
# significantly, your thermal solution is part of your power budget.
```

```systemverilog
// clock_gate.sv - the trunk, not just the leaf.
//
// Register enables (CE) stop the FLOPS toggling. BUFGCE stops the CLOCK TREE
// toggling as well, which the arithmetic above showed is the other half of the
// available saving.
//
// The critical requirement: BUFGCE's enable is sampled internally to guarantee
// a glitch-free output, but the ENABLE ITSELF must be stable and synchronous to
// the clock being gated. A combinational enable produces a runt clock pulse,
// which is a hardware fault, not a timing violation - it will not show up in
// report_timing.

module gated_datapath (
    input  logic        clk,            // ungated source
    input  logic        rst_n,
    input  logic        work_available, // synchronous to clk
    input  logic [31:0] din,
    output logic [31:0] dout
);

    logic clk_gated;
    logic gate_en;

    // Register the enable so BUFGCE never sees a combinational signal.
    // Extend it by a few cycles so the pipeline drains before the clock stops -
    // stopping the clock with data in flight is a classic source of lost beats.
    localparam int DRAIN = 8;
    logic [DRAIN-1:0] drain_sr;

    always_ff @(posedge clk) begin
        if (!rst_n) begin
            drain_sr <= '0;
            gate_en  <= 1'b0;
        end else begin
            drain_sr <= {drain_sr[DRAIN-2:0], work_available};
            gate_en  <= work_available | (|drain_sr);
        end
    end

    BUFGCE u_gate (.I(clk), .CE(gate_en), .O(clk_gated));

    // Everything below is clocked by the gated clock and costs nothing when idle.
    // NOTE: this creates a new clock. Constrain it - Vivado derives a generated
    // clock at the BUFGCE output, but check the name with report_clocks and make
    // sure any crossing between clk and clk_gated is handled (Section 2.3).
    always_ff @(posedge clk_gated) begin
        dout <= din + 32'd1;
    end

endmodule
```

### Final Thoughts on the Budget

Power is the invisible constraint. It dictates your board size, your cooling costs, and your reliability. By using XPE for early estimation, embracing clock gating, and minimizing combinational glitches, you ensure that your design stays within its energy budget. Keep it lean, keep it efficient, and keep it cool.

### Answers

**Brain Power** — *Double the clock frequency: does dynamic power double, quadruple, or
stay the same? Why does voltage matter more?*

**It doubles — linearly.** From $P = \alpha C V^2 f$, power is first-order in $f$:

$$\frac{P_{2f}}{P_f} = \frac{\alpha C V^2 (2f)}{\alpha C V^2 f} = 2$$

Voltage matters more because it appears **squared**:

$$\frac{P_{V/2}}{P_V} = \frac{(V/2)^2}{V^2} = \frac{1}{4}$$

Halving the voltage quarters the power; halving the frequency only halves it. The physical
reason is that $V$ enters twice — the charge moved onto a node is $Q = CV$, and the energy
to move it through a potential difference $V$ is $QV = CV^2$.

The practically important corollary, though, is the one that makes this more than trivia:
**voltage and frequency are coupled.** Lower voltage means slower transistors, so you
cannot take the $V^2$ saving and keep your frequency. That coupling is why dynamic voltage
and frequency scaling reduces *energy per operation* super-linearly — you drop $V$ and $f$
together, giving roughly $V^3$ — and it is why "just lower the voltage" is a system
decision (regulator accuracy, PDN impedance, timing closure at the new corner) rather than
a property you set.

**Sharpen Your Pencil #1** — 8.0 W total, 2.2 W clock network, 40 % duty.

(a) **Register enables only** — the logic gates off, the clock tree keeps running:

$$P = \underbrace{2.2}_{\text{clock tree}} + \underbrace{(8.0 - 2.2) \times 0.40}_{\text{logic}} = 2.2 + 2.32 = 4.52\ \text{W}$$

Saving: $8.0 - 4.52 = 3.48$ W, or **44 %**.

(b) **`BUFGCE` gating the whole domain** — everything stops together:

$$P = 8.0 \times 0.40 = 3.20\ \text{W}$$

Saving: $4.80$ W, or **60 %**.

The extra 1.32 W is the clock tree, and it is 28 % of the original total — worth noticing
that on this module the clock network alone is more than a quarter of the dynamic power.
That ratio rises with fanout and with how spread out the domain is, which connects
straight back to Section 1.3: a clock whose loads span more clock regions than they need
to is burning power on every edge, forever, whether or not the design is doing anything.

**Sharpen Your Pencil #2** — 31.4 W, Confidence "Low", thermal solution for 35 W.

**You do not know whether you are safe, and 31.4 < 35 is not evidence that you are.**
"Low" confidence means the tool used default toggle-rate assumptions rather than your
design's actual activity. The default is a generic assumption; a design with heavy
arithmetic and high data activity can exceed it substantially, and one that is mostly idle
can fall far below. The error bar is easily ±30 %, which spans your 3.6 W of headroom
several times over.

What to do next, in order:

1. **Get a SAIF.** Run a gate-level (or fast functional) simulation of a *representative
   workload* — the thing the product actually does under load, not a reset test or a corner
   case — and feed the SAIF to `report_power`. This is the single step that moves you from
   an assumption to a number.
2. **Set the real operating conditions.** `set_operating_conditions -ambient_temp 55.0`
   (or whatever your chassis actually is) rather than the 25 °C default. Static power rises
   steeply with junction temperature, so the default understates leakage for any real
   enclosure.
3. **Check the split.** If static power is a large fraction, your problem is thermal
   (Section 5.2) and reducing toggling will not help much. If dynamic dominates, the
   clock-gating arithmetic above is where the wins are.
4. **Then re-evaluate the 35 W margin.** And note the direction of the feedback: if the
   thermal solution is marginal, junction temperature rises, leakage rises, power rises.
   Budget power and cooling together or neither number is trustworthy.

**Sharpen Your Pencil #3** — 0.85 V → 0.72 V.

$$\frac{P_{0.72}}{P_{0.85}} = \left(\frac{0.72}{0.85}\right)^2 = (0.847)^2 = 0.718$$

A **28 % reduction in dynamic power** — a genuinely large saving, larger than almost any
RTL change would deliver.

Two costs from earlier chapters:

1. **PDN design gets substantially harder (Section 1.2).** Target impedance is
   $Z = V \times \text{ripple} / \Delta I$. The voltage term falls *and*, because the same
   power now flows at a lower voltage, the current term rises. In the worked example there,
   moving from 0.85 V/20 A to 0.72 V/35 A took the budget from 2.55 mΩ to 0.41 mΩ — over
   6× harder, meaning more capacitors, more copper, and a tighter regulator sense loop.
2. **Timing closure gets harder (Chapters 2 and 4).** Lower voltage means slower
   transistors, so every path's delay increases and your achievable $F_{max}$ drops. A
   design that closed at 400 MHz at 0.85 V may not at 0.72 V, and recovering it costs
   pipelining, area and engineering weeks — which may well consume more than the 28 % was
   worth.

A third, if you want it: regulator accuracy. At 0.72 V a ±3 % tolerance is ±22 mV, and the
device's minimum operating voltage does not scale with your ambition. The sense-point
placement on the board stops being a detail.

The honest summary for a design review: **voltage scaling is the most powerful lever and
the one with the most system-level consequences. It is a board-design decision, made
early, with the PDN and the timing closure plan on the table.**

### There Are No Dumb Questions

**Q: Where does the default toggle rate come from, and can I change it?**

A: Vivado applies default switching activity to nets it has no data for, and you can set
it explicitly with `set_switching_activity` — per net, per hierarchy, or by type
(`-type register`, `-type lut`). Setting it thoughtfully for the parts of your design you
understand well is a reasonable middle path between "accept the default" and "produce a
full SAIF." What is *not* reasonable is quoting the default output as a measurement.

**Q: Is `power_opt_design` safe to run?**

A: Yes — it inserts clock gating that is functionally transparent (the flops it gates were
not going to change value on those cycles anyway). It costs some runtime and can cost a
little timing margin on the gated paths. The usual reason not to run it is that you have
already done better clock gating by hand at the architectural level, where you can gate
whole domains rather than individual registers.

**Q: Does an unused portion of the FPGA consume power?**

A: Static leakage, yes — a large device leaks more than a small one, irrespective of how
much of it you use, and that is a genuine argument for right-sizing the device. Dynamic
power, no: unconfigured fabric does not toggle. This is also why bitstream compression
helps configuration time in proportion to how empty the device is, and why an oversized
device costs you at idle rather than under load.

**Q: My design gets slower when it gets hot. Is that a power problem or a timing problem?**

A: Both, and they are the same problem viewed from two ends. Higher junction temperature
raises leakage (power) and slows transistors (timing), and the tool accounts for the
timing side by analysing at the slow/hot corner. What the tool cannot account for is a
cooling solution that lets the device run hotter than the corner it was signed off at —
so the answer is to make sure your `set_operating_conditions` and your thermal design
agree with each other, which is exactly Section 5.2.

**Q: How much power do the transceivers use?**

A: Enough that they belong in the budget explicitly rather than as an afterthought — GTs
consume power per active lane largely independent of your fabric activity, and a design
with many lanes can find that transceivers dominate. `report_power`'s per-block breakdown
lists them separately; check that line before optimising fabric toggling.

### Bullet Points

- **$P_{\text{dynamic}} = \alpha C V^2 f$.** Frequency and activity are linear levers;
  voltage is quadratic. Voltage and frequency are *coupled*, so you rarely get $V^2$ for
  free.
- **Clock enables save the leaf; `BUFGCE` saves the trunk.** In the worked example, `CE`
  alone captured 50 % of the available saving and gating the clock tree captured 70 %.
- **A gated clock is a new clock.** Constrain it, name it, and handle crossings to the
  ungated domain.
- **Gate with a registered enable, and drain the pipeline first.** A combinational enable
  into `BUFGCE` risks a runt pulse, which `report_timing` will not show you.
- **`report_power` without activity data is an assumption.** Check the **Confidence Level**
  before quoting any number. Feed it a SAIF from a representative workload.
- **Set real operating conditions**: `set_operating_conditions -ambient_temp ...`. The
  25 °C default understates leakage for any real enclosure.
- **Power and thermal are one problem.** Leakage rises with junction temperature, which
  depends on power. Budget both or neither is trustworthy.
- **Register your inputs, not just your outputs**, to stop glitch power at the source.
- **Commands**: `report_power`, `report_power -hierarchical_depth`, `report_power_opt`,
  `power_opt_design`, `read_saif`, `set_switching_activity`, `set_operating_conditions`.
