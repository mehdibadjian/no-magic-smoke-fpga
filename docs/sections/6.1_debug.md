# Section 6.1: Hardware Debugging: The Digital Stethoscope

You’ve simulated your code, you’ve met timing, and you’ve managed the heat. Now comes the moment of truth: you load your bitstream onto the real hardware, and... nothing happens. Or worse, it works for ten minutes and then mysteriously crashes. This is the "Hard Truth" of FPGA design. No matter how much you simulate, there are always bugs that only appear in the real world.

In the AMD UltraFast Methodology, we don't just "guess" what's wrong. we use **Hardware Debugging** tools to look inside the chip. These tools are your "Digital Stethoscope," allowing you to listen to the heartbeat of your design without even touching a physical probe to the board.

### The Digital Stethoscope: Peering Inside

Inside every AMD FPGA, there are dedicated resources for debugging. You can "tap" into any internal signal and bring it out to a virtual dashboard on your computer. This means you can see the state of your state machines, the values in your counters, and the handshakes on your AXI buses—all in real-time, while the chip is running at full speed.

Debugging on hardware is a superpower. It allows you to catch the "Ghost Bugs" that only appear when the real world gets messy. But remember: every signal you debug uses a tiny bit of the FPGA's resources. Use your stethoscope wisely.

> **Brain Power**
> **If you add a 1024-sample ILA to a 64-bit bus, how many bits of BRAM are you consuming? If your FPGA is already 95% full, will you still be able to add a large debug core?**

### The ILA: Your High-Speed Waveform Capture

The **Integrated Logic Analyzer (ILA)** is the workhorse of FPGA debugging. It’s a piece of IP that you drop into your design. It "samples" your signals on every clock edge and stores the data in the FPGA’s internal BRAM. When a specific "Trigger Condition" is met (like an error flag going high), the ILA sends that data back to Vivado, where it’s displayed as a waveform.

Think of the ILA as a high-speed security camera. It’s always watching, but it only "saves" the footage when it sees something suspicious. By setting the right trigger, you can capture the exact moment your logic fails, even if it only happens once every three hours.

### Fireside Chat: The Lab Tech and the Remote Engineer

**Lab Tech:** "I’ve been trying to trigger on this 'Packet Error' for two hours. I’ve got the oscilloscope probes hooked up to the output pins, but I can't see what's happening inside the FPGA. I think the bug is in the CRC calculation."

**Remote Engineer:** "Stop wasting your time with probes. I’ve just instantiated an ILA core on the CRC logic. I’m sitting in my home office, five hundred miles away, and I can see the exact state of the CRC registers on my screen. Look at this... the third bit is flipping incorrectly because of a timing violation."

**Lab Tech:** "Wait, you can see that from home? Without any probes?"

**Remote Engineer:** "Yep. JTAG-over-IP. The ILA is my eyes and ears. I don't need to be in the lab to find a logic bug. I just need a network connection and a good trigger condition. Welcome to the future of debugging."

### The VIO: Pushing Buttons from Your Keyboard

Sometimes you don't need to see high-speed waveforms; you just need to "push a button" or "flip a switch" inside the chip. That’s what the **Virtual I/O (VIO)** core is for.

The VIO allows you to create virtual buttons and sliders in the Vivado dashboard. You can use them to reset a specific module, change a configuration register, or force an error condition. It’s like having a physical control panel for your FPGA, but it lives entirely on your computer screen. VIOs are great for "Sanity Checking" your design during the first few hours of board bring-up.

### Debugging Strategies: Finding the Needle

The biggest challenge with hardware debugging is the **Data Limit**. Because the ILA uses internal BRAM, you can only capture a few thousand samples at a time. If your bug happens over a long period, you’ll never see it in a single capture.

To find the "Needle in the Haystack," you need a clever strategy:

1.  **Iterative Triggers:** Start with a broad trigger and narrow it down as you learn more.
2.  **State Machine Debugging:** Bring the 'Current State' signal into the ILA. It’s the single most useful signal for finding logic errors.
3.  **Capture Control:** Use the "Storage Qualification" feature to only capture data when a specific condition is met (like `valid == 1`). This allows you to "stretch" your small BRAM buffer across a long period.

### Code Detective: The ILA Clock Trap

You must connect your ILA core to a clock signal. If you pick the wrong one, your debug data will be garbage.

```tcl
# Code Detective: Why will this ILA produce confusing results?
# The design has a 400MHz data path and a 10MHz control path.
# The user connects the ILA to the 10MHz clock but probes the 400MHz signals.
# Hint: What happens when you sample a fast signal with a slow clock?
```

The flaw here is **Under-sampling**. If you sample a 400MHz signal with a 10MHz clock, you're only seeing one out of every forty transitions.

But under-sampling is the *benign* half of the problem. The dangerous half is that the ILA
is now performing an **unsynchronized clock domain crossing on every probe**. The 400 MHz
signals are asynchronous to the 10 MHz sample clock, so each captured bit can go metastable
independently — and a multi-bit bus captured this way tears exactly as described in
Section 2.3. **The waveform you are shown will contain values that never existed in the
design**, and you will spend a day debugging them.

**The fix:**

```tcl
# Corrected: one ILA per clock domain, each clocked by the domain it probes.

# --- Fast domain ---------------------------------------------------------------
create_debug_core u_ila_400 ila
set_property C_DATA_DEPTH  1024      [get_debug_cores u_ila_400]
set_property C_TRIGIN_EN   false     [get_debug_cores u_ila_400]
set_property C_ADV_TRIGGER true      [get_debug_cores u_ila_400]   ;# multi-state trigger
connect_debug_port u_ila_400/clk [get_nets clk_400]

create_debug_port u_ila_400 probe
set_property PROBE_TYPE DATA_AND_TRIGGER [get_debug_ports u_ila_400/probe0]
set_property PORT_WIDTH 64               [get_debug_ports u_ila_400/probe0]
connect_debug_port u_ila_400/probe0 [get_nets [list {dsp_result[*]}]]

# --- Slow domain ---------------------------------------------------------------
create_debug_core u_ila_10 ila
set_property C_DATA_DEPTH 4096       [get_debug_cores u_ila_10]
connect_debug_port u_ila_10/clk [get_nets clk_10]

# --- Correlate them with cross-triggering rather than a shared clock ------------
# This is what you actually wanted: one domain's event triggering the other's
# capture, WITHOUT sampling one domain's signals on the other's clock.
set_property C_TRIGOUT_EN true [get_debug_cores u_ila_400]
set_property C_TRIGIN_EN  true [get_debug_cores u_ila_10]
```

If you genuinely need a fast-domain signal visible in a slow-domain capture, cross it
properly first — with an `xpm_cdc_single` or `xpm_cdc_handshake` — and probe the
*synchronized* version. You lose timing detail, which is honest, instead of gaining
fictional values, which is not.

### Analogy → Silicon

| In the story | In the silicon | Where the analogy breaks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Digital stethoscope | The **ILA** core: probes, a trigger comparator, and a BRAM capture buffer | A stethoscope is passive; an ILA consumes BRAM, LUTs and routing, and inserting one *changes the design you are debugging* |
| Security camera saving only suspicious footage | Trigger conditions plus **capture control** / storage qualification | A camera's storage is effectively unlimited; an ILA's buffer is a handful of BRAMs, and the arithmetic below decides what you can see |
| Virtual control panel | The **VIO** core: fabric-driven inputs and outputs, updated over JTAG | Real panel switches are instantaneous; VIO updates are asynchronous to your design and arrive whenever JTAG gets round to it — never use a VIO output as a synchronous control without synchronizing it |
| Watching a race by blinking | Sampling one clock domain with another domain's clock | Blinking loses information; this *fabricates* information, because an unsynchronized multi-bit capture shows values that never existed |
| Finding a needle | Iterative trigger refinement and multi-state trigger sequences | Needles are static; the bug moves as you add probes, because probes change placement, routing and timing |

### Do the Math

#### What an ILA costs, and therefore what you can afford to see

The capture buffer is real BRAM, and the arithmetic is exact:

$$\text{Storage bits} = \text{probe width (total)} \times \text{capture depth}$$

$$\text{BRAMs} = \left\lceil \frac{\text{Storage bits}}{36{,}864} \right\rceil \quad (\text{one RAMB36E2} = 36\ \text{Kb})$$

**Givens:**

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Probes | one 64-bit bus, one 32-bit counter, 8 single-bit control signals |
| Capture depth | 1024 samples |

$$W_{\text{total}} = 64 + 32 + 8 = 104\ \text{bits}$$

$$\text{Storage} = 104 \times 1024 = 106{,}496\ \text{bits} = 104\ \text{Kb}$$

$$\text{BRAMs} = \left\lceil \frac{106{,}496}{36{,}864} \right\rceil = \lceil 2.89 \rceil = 3\ \text{BRAMs}$$

Now the number that changes behaviour. Ask for a deeper window:

| Depth | Storage | BRAMs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1,024 | 104 Kb | 3 |
| 8,192 | 832 Kb | 23 |
| 32,768 | 3,328 Kb | 91 |
| 131,072 | 13,312 Kb | 362 |

**Depth is linear in BRAM, and BRAM is the resource you are most likely to be short of.**
On a device with 600 BRAMs where your design already uses 480, a 32K-deep capture of 104
bits is affordable and a 128K-deep one is not, regardless of how much you would like it.

#### How long a window is that, really?

$$T_{\text{window}} = \frac{\text{depth}}{f_{\text{sample}}}$$

At 400 MHz:

$$\frac{1024}{400\times10^{6}} = 2.56\ \mu\text{s}$$

**2.56 microseconds.** If your bug occurs once every three hours, the probability that a
blind capture contains it is:

$$\frac{2.56\times10^{-6}}{10{,}800} = 2.4\times10^{-10}$$

Which is the entire argument for triggering: **you are not capturing more, you are
capturing at the right moment.** And it is also the argument for *storage qualification* —
if the interesting events are the 1 % of cycles where `valid` is high, qualifying the
capture on `valid` stretches the same 1024 samples across 100× more real time:

$$T_{\text{effective}} \approx \frac{1024}{0.01 \times 400\times10^{6}} = 256\ \mu\text{s}$$

Same three BRAMs, a hundred times the coverage.

### The Real Artifact

The debug flow that keeps the probes out of your RTL, plus a VIO used correctly.

```tcl
#==============================================================================
# debug.tcl - insert debug on the synthesised netlist, not in the source.
#
# Why not `(* mark_debug *)` in RTL? Because attributes in source get committed,
# forgotten, and shipped. Netlist-based insertion lives in a script you can
# exclude from a production build with one flag.
#
# Usage:
#   synth_design ...
#   source debug.tcl            ;# only when DEBUG_BUILD is set
#   opt_design; place_design; route_design
#==============================================================================

if {![info exists ::env(DEBUG_BUILD)]} {
    puts "INFO: production build - no debug cores inserted."
    return
}

#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 1. An ILA per clock domain. Never share a clock between domains - see the
#    Code Detective above for what that actually does to your waveforms.
#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
proc add_ila {name clk_net depth probes} {
    create_debug_core $name ila
    set_property C_DATA_DEPTH   $depth [get_debug_cores $name]
    set_property C_TRIGIN_EN    true   [get_debug_cores $name]
    set_property C_TRIGOUT_EN   true   [get_debug_cores $name]
    set_property C_ADV_TRIGGER  true   [get_debug_cores $name]
    set_property C_EN_STRG_QUAL true   [get_debug_cores $name]   ;# capture control
    set_property ALL_PROBE_SAME_MU true [get_debug_cores $name]
    connect_debug_port $name/clk [get_nets $clk_net]

    set idx 0
    foreach {pattern width} $probes {
        if {$idx > 0} { create_debug_port $name probe }
        set port "$name/probe$idx"
        set_property PROBE_TYPE DATA_AND_TRIGGER [get_debug_ports $port]
        set_property PORT_WIDTH $width           [get_debug_ports $port]
        connect_debug_port $port [get_nets [list $pattern]]
        incr idx
    }
}

# The single most useful probe in any design is the state machine's state vector.
# Probe it first, every time. Most "impossible" behaviour is a state machine in a
# state you did not know it could reach.
add_ila u_ila_400 clk_400 1024 {
    {axi_state[*]}      4
    {dsp_result[*]}    64
    {sample_valid}      1
    {fifo_full}         1
    {fifo_empty}        1
}

#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 2. Capture control - the technique from "Do the Math" that buys 100x the
#    time window for free. Set the storage qualifier in the hardware manager:
#      set_property CONTROL.CAPTURE_MODE BASIC [get_hw_ilas hw_ila_1]
#      set_property CONTROL.TRIGGER_POSITION 512 [get_hw_ilas hw_ila_1]
#    TRIGGER_POSITION in the middle gives you PRE-trigger history, which is
#    usually where the cause is - the trigger is the symptom.
#------------------------------------------------------------------------------

#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Keep the probed nets alive through implementation.
#    Without this, opt_design may merge or remove the very net you are probing.
#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
set_property MARK_DEBUG true [get_nets -hier -filter {NAME =~ "*axi_state*"}]

#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 4. Save the probe file. Without it the hardware manager shows probe0, probe1...
#    with no names, and you will spend the session counting bits.
#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
write_debug_probes -force ./build/debug_nets.ltx
```

```systemverilog
// vio_control.sv - a VIO used safely.
//
// The trap: VIO outputs change whenever the JTAG chain gets round to updating
// them. They are asynchronous to your fabric clock, and they are NOT glitch free
// across a multi-bit field. Treat every VIO output as an asynchronous input from
// another chip, because functionally that is exactly what it is.

module vio_control (
    input  logic        clk,
    input  logic        rst_n,
    output logic        soft_reset,
    output logic [7:0]  test_mode
);

    logic       vio_reset_async;
    logic [7:0] vio_mode_async;

    vio_0 u_vio (
        .clk        (clk),
        .probe_out0 (vio_reset_async),
        .probe_out1 (vio_mode_async)
    );

    // Single bit: a plain synchronizer is enough.
    xpm_cdc_single #(.DEST_SYNC_FF(3), .SRC_INPUT_REG(0)) u_sync_rst (
        .src_clk (1'b0), .src_in (vio_reset_async),
        .dest_clk(clk),  .dest_out(soft_reset)
    );

    // Multi-bit COHERENT field: independent synchronizers would let it tear,
    // and a torn test_mode is a test mode you never selected. Handshake it.
    xpm_cdc_handshake #(
        .WIDTH(8), .DEST_SYNC_FF(3), .SRC_SYNC_FF(3), .DEST_EXT_HSK(0)
    ) u_sync_mode (
        .src_clk (clk), .src_in (vio_mode_async), .src_send(1'b1), .src_rcv(),
        .dest_clk(clk), .dest_out(test_mode),     .dest_req(), .dest_ack(1'b0)
    );

endmodule
```

### Sharpen Your Pencil

1. You want to probe a 256-bit AXI data bus plus 12 control signals at 8,192 samples deep.
   Compute the storage and the BRAM cost. Your design has 92 BRAMs free. What do you do?
2. Your bug appears roughly once an hour. Your capture window is 1024 samples at 250 MHz.
   Compute the window duration and the probability a blind capture catches it. Name the
   two techniques that change this and say which one to try first.
3. You add an ILA to investigate a timing-related failure and the failure disappears.
   Give the two most likely explanations, and say how you would investigate without the
   ILA.

### Final Thoughts on the Stethoscope

Hardware debugging is where the "Digital Dream" meets the "Physical Reality." It requires patience, logic, and the right tools. By mastering the ILA and the VIO, and by using clever triggering strategies, you can peel back the layers of the silicon and see exactly what your logic is doing. Find the ghosts, fix the bugs, and get your design to the finish line.

### Answers

**Brain Power** — *A 1024-sample ILA on a 64-bit bus: how many BRAM bits? And can you add
one at 95 % utilisation?*

$$\text{Storage} = 64\ \text{bits} \times 1024\ \text{samples} = 65{,}536\ \text{bits} = 64\ \text{Kb}$$

$$\text{BRAMs} = \left\lceil \frac{65{,}536}{36{,}864} \right\rceil = \lceil 1.78 \rceil = 2\ \text{RAMB36E2}$$

Two BRAMs — and note the rounding: you asked for 1.78 and you pay for 2. The ILA's own
trigger comparators, control logic and JTAG interface cost LUTs and flops on top of that,
typically a few hundred of each per core.

**At 95 % utilisation, the honest answer is: probably, but not usefully.** Two BRAMs may
well exist. The problem is everything else:

- **Routing.** Probes are nets from wherever the signal lives to wherever the ILA lands.
  At 95 % utilisation the routing resources to carry 64 bits across the die may simply not
  be there, and the design will fail to route.
- **Timing.** Adding an ILA changes placement, which changes routing, which changes every
  path in the neighbourhood. At 95 % utilisation a design that closed at +0.020 ns will
  very likely not close with an ILA in it.
- **The observer effect.** If it does close, it closes *differently* — so you may be
  debugging a design that no longer exhibits the bug (see *Sharpen Your Pencil #3*).

What to do instead when the device is nearly full: probe fewer signals (a state vector and
two control bits beat a 64-bit bus for most bugs), use a shallower buffer with better
triggering, or — the technique people forget — **remove functionality temporarily.** A
build with one processing channel instead of eight has room for instrumentation and
usually reproduces the bug.

**Sharpen Your Pencil #1** — 256 + 12 = 268 bits, 8,192 deep:

$$\text{Storage} = 268 \times 8192 = 2{,}195{,}456\ \text{bits} = 2{,}144\ \text{Kb}$$

$$\text{BRAMs} = \left\lceil \frac{2{,}195{,}456}{36{,}864} \right\rceil = \lceil 59.6 \rceil = 60\ \text{BRAMs}$$

You have 92 free, so it **fits** — using 65 % of your remaining BRAM on debug.

Whether you *should* is a different question, and the answer is usually no. What to do
instead:

- **Do you need all 256 data bits?** For a protocol or state bug, the control signals,
  the `TLAST`/`TKEEP`, and 32 bits of data are almost always enough to find it. Dropping to
  44 bits total takes you to 10 BRAMs.
- **Do you need 8,192 samples?** With a good trigger, 1,024 usually suffices. Depth
  compensates for a bad trigger; a good trigger is cheaper.
- **Use storage qualification** on `TVALID` — most AXI-Stream buses are idle most of the
  time, and qualifying discards the idle cycles for free.

A reasonable final answer: 44 bits × 2,048 samples with capture control ≈ 3 BRAMs, and it
will show you more useful cycles than the 60-BRAM version.

**Sharpen Your Pencil #2** — 1024 samples at 250 MHz:

$$T_{\text{window}} = \frac{1024}{250\times10^{6}} = 4.096\ \mu\text{s}$$

$$P(\text{catch}) = \frac{4.096\times10^{-6}}{3600} = 1.14\times10^{-9}$$

About one in 880 million. You would be capturing for roughly 28 years to expect one hit.

The two techniques:

1. **Triggering.** Configure the ILA to arm on the condition that accompanies the failure —
   an error flag, an unexpected state, a FIFO overflow, a protocol violation. This changes
   the problem from "sample randomly" to "sample at the moment of interest," and it takes
   $P$ from $10^{-9}$ to 1.
2. **Storage qualification / capture control.** Discard uninteresting cycles so the same
   buffer covers more real time.

**Try triggering first**, and it is not close. Storage qualification multiplies your window
by perhaps 100×, taking $10^{-9}$ to $10^{-7}$ — still hopeless. Triggering makes the
capture deterministic. Qualification is what you add *afterwards*, to get more context
around a trigger you have already got working.

If you cannot define a trigger — "it just goes wrong eventually" — that is itself the
finding: build one. Add an assertion in fabric (a checker that compares against an expected
invariant) and trigger the ILA on the assertion firing. That converts an unobservable
intermittent bug into a reproducible capture, and it is the single highest-value debug
technique in this section.

**Sharpen Your Pencil #3** — Adding an ILA makes the failure disappear.

The two most likely explanations:

1. **The insertion changed placement and routing, and the timing-marginal path moved.**
   The ILA displaced logic, the placer rearranged the neighbourhood, and the path that was
   failing by 20 ps now has 50 ps of slack. The bug is still in the design — it is in a
   *different* path now, or it is waiting for the next build to reappear. This is the
   overwhelmingly common case.
2. **`MARK_DEBUG` prevented an optimisation that was itself the bug.** Probing a net stops
   it being merged, retimed or absorbed. If the failure depended on that optimisation — say,
   a net being merged with another that had different timing — preserving it removes the
   symptom.

How to investigate without the ILA:

- **Static timing analysis, properly.** `report_timing_summary -check_timing_verbose
  -report_unconstrained` on the *non-ILA* build. A failure that changes with placement is
  a timing failure, and STA analyses every path rather than the handful you can probe. Look
  hardest at what `check_timing` says is *unconstrained*.
- **`report_cdc -details`.** Failures that appear and disappear with placement are very
  often CDC, because routing changes alter the resolution time in the MTBF equation
  (Section 2.3). CDC failures also never show up in WNS, so a green timing report does not
  clear them.
- **Build variance as a signal.** Implement the design several times with different
  directives or `-seed` values. If the failure comes and goes across builds, you have
  confirmed a marginality rather than a logic bug, and you should stop probing and start
  fixing margin.
- **Non-invasive observation.** Add a counter that increments on the error condition and
  read it over an existing interface (AXI-Lite register, UART, even a `VIO` input, which
  is far cheaper than an ILA). You lose the waveform and keep the design intact — often the
  right trade for a marginality.

The meta-lesson: **when instrumentation changes the behaviour, the instrumentation has
told you something.** "It disappears when I add an ILA" is not a dead end; it is strong
evidence that the bug is timing or CDC marginality rather than logic, and that redirects
the whole investigation.

### There Are No Dumb Questions

**Q: `MARK_DEBUG` in RTL, or `create_debug_core` in a Tcl script?**

A: The script, for anything beyond a quick experiment. RTL attributes get committed and
forgotten, and eventually one ships — blocking optimisation in a production build for a
debug session someone finished a year ago. Netlist insertion lives in a file you can gate
on an environment variable, as in the artifact above. Use RTL attributes when you need to
probe a signal that optimisation would otherwise delete before you can reach it.

**Q: Why does my ILA show `X` on some probes?**

A: Usually because the net was optimised away and the probe is connected to nothing, or
because the design has not come out of reset in that domain. Check the implementation log
for messages about debug nets being removed, and confirm the ILA's clock is actually
running — an ILA on a gated clock (Section 5.1) captures nothing while the clock is
stopped, which looks identical to a dead design.

**Q: Can I leave debug cores in a production bitstream?**

A: You can, and there are two reasons to think carefully. It costs resources and timing
margin permanently. More importantly, it leaves a JTAG-accessible window into your
design's internal state, which is a security consideration if you have gone to the trouble
of encrypting the bitstream (Section 3.3). Some products deliberately keep a minimal ILA
for field diagnosis; that is a decision to make explicitly, not by forgetting to remove it.

**Q: What is the single most useful signal to probe?**

A: The state vector of whatever state machine governs the failing behaviour. Most
"impossible" hardware behaviour resolves to a state machine sitting in a state you did not
know it could reach, or cycling between two states because a condition never clears. Probe
`state` first, add data later.

**Q: The bug only happens in the field, not on my bench. Now what?**

A: Instrument for *statistics* rather than waveforms. Counters for error events, a
minimum/maximum tracker for a FIFO occupancy, a sticky flag for each error class, all
readable over an existing management interface. Sticky and cumulative beats a waveform you
will never be present to capture — the same argument as the sticky alarms in Sections 1.2
and 5.2. Then use the field data to build a bench reproduction.

### Bullet Points

- **ILA storage** $=$ total probe width × depth. **One `RAMB36E2` = 36 Kb.** Round up.
- 104 bits × 1024 = **3 BRAMs**. 268 bits × 8192 = **60 BRAMs**. Depth is linear and
  expensive.
- **Window duration** $=$ depth $/ f_{\text{sample}}$. 1024 at 400 MHz is **2.56 µs** — so
  a blind capture of an hourly bug is hopeless. **Trigger first, qualify second.**
- **Storage qualification** stretches the same buffer over ~100× more time on a bus that is
  idle 99 % of the time.
- **Set `TRIGGER_POSITION` mid-buffer** so you capture the cause, not just the symptom.
- **One ILA per clock domain.** Sampling a fast domain with a slow clock is an
  unsynchronized CDC that fabricates values. Cross-trigger between cores instead.
- **VIO outputs are asynchronous.** Synchronize single bits with `xpm_cdc_single`, coherent
  fields with `xpm_cdc_handshake`.
- **Probe the state vector first.** It finds more bugs than any data bus.
- **If the bug disappears when you add an ILA**, you have learned it is timing or CDC
  marginality. Switch to `report_timing_summary -check_timing_verbose` and `report_cdc`.
- **Commands**: `create_debug_core`, `create_debug_port`, `connect_debug_port`,
  `set_property MARK_DEBUG`, `write_debug_probes`, and `CONTROL.CAPTURE_MODE` /
  `CONTROL.TRIGGER_POSITION` in the hardware manager.
