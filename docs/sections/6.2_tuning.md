# Section 6.2: System Performance Tuning: The Final Sprint

Congratulations! Your FPGA design is running, it’s stable, and it’s cool. But there’s one final hurdle before you can ship: **Performance Tuning**. In the UltraFast methodology, meeting your timing constraints is just the minimum requirement. To truly succeed, your system must meet its "Real-World" performance goals, whether that’s processing 100 gigabits of data per second or responding to a radar pulse in under a microsecond.

Think of performance tuning like a Formula 1 pit crew. The car is already fast, but the crew is looking for those tiny adjustments—the tire pressure, the wing angle, the fuel mix—that will turn a fast car into a championship-winning one.

### Throughput vs. Latency: Choosing Your Race

In system tuning, you are usually optimizing for one of two things:

1.  **Throughput:** The total amount of data you can process in a given time (e.g., Megabytes per second). This is like a massive cargo train—it carries a lot of weight, but it takes a long time to start and stop.
2.  **Latency:** The time it takes for a single piece of data to travel from input to output (e.g., nanoseconds). This is like a high-speed drone—it carries very little, but it’s incredibly fast and agile.

The AMD UltraFast methodology teaches you that you often have to trade one for the other. Adding registers (pipelining) increases your throughput by allowing higher clock speeds, but it also increases your latency by adding clock cycles to the path. Know your "Race" and tune accordingly.

> **Brain Power**
> **In a High-Frequency Trading (HFT) system, which is more important: Throughput or Latency? Why would adding an extra register to a 400MHz path actually HURT an HFT strategy even if it makes the timing easier to meet?**

### AXI Performance Monitor (APM): The Data Traffic Camera

Most modern AMD FPGA designs use the **AXI Interconnect** to move data between modules. If your system is slow, the problem is usually a "Data Traffic Jam" in the interconnect. The **AXI Performance Monitor (APM)** is your tool for diagnosing these jams.

The APM tracks how many bytes are moving across each bus and how much time the data spends waiting for a "Ready" signal. It’s like a traffic camera on a busy highway. If the APM shows that your DDR4 memory is only 20% utilized, but your processor is 100% busy, you know the bottleneck isn't the memory—it's the processing logic. Don't guess where the bottleneck is; let the APM show you the numbers.

### Fireside Chat: The Bandwidth King and the Latency Ninja

**Bandwidth King:** "I’ve optimized the DMA engine to move 512 bits per clock cycle! We can fill up the entire DDR4 memory in less than a second. My throughput is legendary!"

**Latency Ninja:** "That’s impressive, but your DMA engine takes 2,000 clock cycles just to start a transfer. By the time your first byte arrives, my radar system has already missed the target. Your cargo train is too slow to react."

**Bandwidth King:** "React? Who cares about reaction time when you have this much volume?"

**Latency Ninja:** "The customer cares. They need the answer *now*, not a gigabyte of data later. I’ve tuned my design for a single-cycle response. I move less data, but I move it instantly. In my world, the first byte is the only one that matters."

### Bottleneck Identification: Finding the Slowest Runner

Every system has a **Bottleneck**—the single component that limits the overall performance. It might be the DDR4 bandwidth, the PCIe latency, or a poorly written state machine in your RTL.

To find the bottleneck, use a "Stress Test." Gradually increase the data rate until the system fails. Then, use the ILA and APM to see which module was the first to "choke." Is a FIFO overflowing? Is a bus master waiting too long for an arbiter? Once you find the "Slowest Runner," you can focus 100% of your energy on optimizing that one component. Improving anything else is a waste of time.

### The Victory Lap: Final Validation

The "Victory Lap" is the final step of the UltraFast methodology. It’s where you run your design under "Worst-Case" conditions—maximum data rate, highest temperature, and minimum voltage—and verify that it still meets all its goals.

Document your performance results. Create a "Benchmark Report" that shows your throughput, latency, and resource utilization. This report isn't just for the customer; it’s for *you*. It’s your proof that you followed the methodology, you did the work, and you built a world-class FPGA system.

### Code Detective: The FIFO Starvation Trap

A common performance killer is a FIFO that isn't sized correctly for the system latency.

```verilog
// Code Detective: Why will this system fail to reach its maximum throughput?
// The AXI master has a latency of 100 clock cycles to get data.
// The FIFO is only 64 entries deep.
// The consumer reads one entry per clock cycle.
// Hint: Can the master refill the FIFO before the consumer empties it?
```

The flaw here is **FIFO Starvation**. If the latency to get new data (100 cycles) is longer than the time it takes to empty the FIFO (64 cycles), the consumer will run out of data and have to wait.

Put a number on the loss, because "throughput will drop significantly" is not actionable.
The consumer drains 64 entries in 64 cycles, then waits 36 cycles for the refill to arrive:

$$\text{Duty} = \frac{64}{64 + 36} = \frac{64}{100} = 64\ \%$$

**You are getting 64 % of the throughput you designed for**, and no amount of tuning the
memory controller changes it, because the memory controller is not the problem.

**The fix**, with the sizing derived rather than guessed:

$$\text{Depth} \ge \text{round-trip latency} \times \text{consumption rate}$$

$$\text{Depth} \ge 100\ \text{cycles} \times 1\ \text{entry/cycle} = 100\ \text{entries}$$

Round up to a power of two and add margin for jitter in the memory latency, which is
never actually constant:

```systemverilog
// 128 entries: 100 for the latency-bandwidth product, 28 for latency variation.
// Note this is the SAME calculation as TCP's bandwidth-delay product, and the same
// as a CPU prefetch distance - it is the general answer to "how much buffering does
// a pipeline with latency need?"
xpm_fifo_sync #(
    .FIFO_MEMORY_TYPE ("block"),
    .FIFO_WRITE_DEPTH (128),
    .WRITE_DATA_WIDTH (512),
    .READ_DATA_WIDTH  (512),
    .READ_MODE        ("fwft"),     // first-word-fall-through: removes a cycle of
                                    // read latency from the consumer's critical loop
    .PROG_EMPTY_THRESH(32)          // request the next burst at 32 remaining, NOT at
                                    // empty - requesting at empty guarantees a stall
) u_prefetch_fifo (...);
```

The `PROG_EMPTY_THRESH` line is the half people miss. A correctly sized FIFO that only
requests more data when it hits *empty* still stalls for the full round trip. Issue the
next request while there is still enough data buffered to cover the latency:

$$\text{Threshold} \ge \text{round-trip latency} \times \text{consumption rate} = 100$$

which for a 128-deep FIFO means requesting at 100 remaining, not 32 — or, more practically,
keeping **two requests in flight** so the pipeline never has a gap. That last technique,
issuing the next request before the current one lands, is what actually saturates a
high-latency memory, and it is why AXI supports multiple outstanding transactions at all.

### Analogy → Silicon

| In the story | In the silicon | Where the analogy breaks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cargo train vs high-speed drone | Throughput (bytes/s) vs latency (ns from input to output) | Trains and drones are different vehicles; throughput and latency are properties of *one* pipeline, traded against each other by the same design choices |
| Traffic camera on the highway | **AXI Performance Monitor**: transaction counts, and cycles spent waiting on `READY` | A camera counts vehicles; the APM's most valuable output is not the count but the **stall time**, which is what identifies the bottleneck |
| The slowest runner | The bottleneck stage, whose throughput bounds the whole pipeline | Runners can be substituted; a bottleneck in FPGA design often *moves* once you fix it, so the process is iterative by nature |
| Pit crew adjustments | `PROG_EMPTY_THRESH`, outstanding transaction depth, burst length, FIFO depth | Pit adjustments are independent; these interact, and changing burst length alters the latency that determined your FIFO depth |
| The victory lap | Validation at worst-case data rate, temperature and voltage | A victory lap is ceremonial; this is where systems fail, because every earlier measurement was taken under convenient conditions |

### Do the Math

#### Little's Law: the one formula that sizes every buffer you will ever build

$$L = \lambda \times W$$

Items in flight $=$ arrival rate $\times$ time in system. Everything below is a special
case.

**Case 1 — FIFO depth for a latency-hiding prefetch.**

**Givens:**

| Symbol | Meaning | Value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\lambda$ | Consumption rate | 1 beat/cycle |
| $W$ | Memory round-trip latency | 140 cycles |

$$L = 1 \times 140 = 140\ \text{beats minimum}$$

Round up to 256 for headroom and power-of-two addressing. A 64-deep FIFO here delivers
$64/140 = 46\ \%$ of line rate, and the memory subsystem will look innocent throughout.

**Case 2 — outstanding transactions to saturate a link.**

$$N_{\text{outstanding}} \ge \frac{\text{round-trip latency}}{\text{burst duration}}$$

**Givens:**

| Symbol | Meaning | Value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Round-trip latency | Request issued to first data returned | 140 cycles |
| Burst length | AXI beats per transaction | 16 beats |
| Burst duration | Time to transfer one burst | 16 cycles |

$$N \ge \frac{140}{16} = 8.75 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad 9 \text{ outstanding transactions}$$

If your master supports 4, you achieve at most:

$$\frac{4 \times 16}{140} = 46\ \%\ \text{of line rate}$$

**Same 46 %, from a completely different cause.** This is why "the DDR is slow" is almost
never the diagnosis — a memory controller delivering 19.2 GB/s to a master that can only
keep four bursts in flight will report itself as mostly idle while the system starves.

#### Turning an APM reading into a verdict

The APM gives you transaction counts and stall cycles. Convert them:

$$\text{Efficiency} = \frac{\text{data beats transferred}}{\text{total cycles observed}}$$

**Givens** — one observation window:

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Observation window | 1,000,000 cycles |
| Data beats transferred | 410,000 |
| Cycles with `VALID` high and `READY` low (slave stalling master) | 62,000 |
| Cycles with `READY` high and `VALID` low (master starving slave) | 528,000 |

$$\text{Efficiency} = \frac{410{,}000}{1{,}000{,}000} = 41\ \%$$

Now the diagnosis, which is in the *ratio of the two stall types*:

- Slave stalling the master: 62,000 cycles (6 %)
- Master starving the slave: **528,000 cycles (53 %)**

**The slave is idle and waiting more than half the time.** The bottleneck is upstream — the
master is not issuing requests fast enough, which points straight at outstanding
transaction depth or FIFO sizing, i.e. Cases 1 and 2 above. Had the numbers been reversed,
the memory subsystem would have been the constraint and you would be looking at burst
length, address patterns and bank conflicts.

**Verdict: measure which side is waiting before optimising either.** Half of all
performance work is spent optimising the component that was already fast enough.

#### The throughput/latency trade, quantified

Adding a pipeline stage:

$$f_{\max} \uparrow, \qquad \text{latency} = N_{\text{stages}} \times T_{\text{clk}}$$

**Givens:** a path closing at 250 MHz with 4 stages, versus 400 MHz with 7 stages.

$$\text{Latency}_{250} = \frac{4}{250\times10^{6}} = 16.0\ \text{ns}$$

$$\text{Latency}_{400} = \frac{7}{400\times10^{6}} = 17.5\ \text{ns}$$

Throughput improved 60 % (250 → 400 MHz) and latency got **worse by 1.5 ns**. For a
streaming application that is an obvious win. For a system whose specification is
"respond within 20 ns," you have just spent 9 % of the entire budget to gain throughput
you may not need.

### The Real Artifact

Instrumentation that answers "where is the time going" without an APM licence, plus the
measurement discipline that makes the numbers mean something.

```systemverilog
// axi_stall_counters.sv
//
// A minimal, always-on performance monitor. Four counters per interface tell you
// everything the expensive tools tell you about WHERE a pipeline is waiting:
//
//   beats      - useful work done
//   stall_slave- VALID && !READY : the downstream cannot keep up
//   stall_mstr - !VALID && READY : the upstream cannot keep up
//   idle       - !VALID && !READY: nothing wanted anything
//
// The RATIO of stall_slave to stall_mstr is the diagnosis. Leave these in the
// production design - they cost a few hundred flops and they turn a customer
// report of "it's slow" into a number.

module axi_stall_counters #(
    parameter int CW = 48                 // wide enough not to wrap during a run
) (
    input  logic clk,
    input  logic rst_n,
    input  logic tvalid,
    input  logic tready,
    input  logic sample_en,               // gate to one measurement window
    input  logic clear,

    output logic [CW-1:0] beats,
    output logic [CW-1:0] stall_slave,    // producer ready, consumer not
    output logic [CW-1:0] stall_master,   // consumer ready, producer not
    output logic [CW-1:0] idle,
    output logic [CW-1:0] window_cycles
);

    always_ff @(posedge clk) begin
        if (!rst_n || clear) begin
            beats         <= '0;
            stall_slave   <= '0;
            stall_master  <= '0;
            idle          <= '0;
            window_cycles <= '0;
        end else if (sample_en) begin
            window_cycles <= window_cycles + 1'b1;
            unique case ({tvalid, tready})
                2'b11: beats        <= beats        + 1'b1;   // transfer
                2'b10: stall_slave  <= stall_slave  + 1'b1;   // backpressure
                2'b01: stall_master <= stall_master + 1'b1;   // starvation
                2'b00: idle         <= idle         + 1'b1;   // nothing offered
            endcase
        end
    end

endmodule
```

```tcl
#==============================================================================
# perf_measure.tcl - the discipline that makes a benchmark mean something.
#
# The three mistakes this exists to prevent:
#   1. Measuring at nominal conditions and shipping against worst case.
#   2. Measuring one workload and reporting it as "the" throughput.
#   3. Optimising before measuring which side of the interface is waiting.
#==============================================================================

# --- 1. Read the counters over the existing debug or management path ----------
proc read_perf {ila} {
    set beats  [get_hw_probe_value ${ila}/beats]
    set st_slv [get_hw_probe_value ${ila}/stall_slave]
    set st_mst [get_hw_probe_value ${ila}/stall_master]
    set total  [get_hw_probe_value ${ila}/window_cycles]

    set eff [expr {100.0 * $beats / $total}]
    puts [format "efficiency        %.1f%%"  $eff]
    puts [format "backpressure      %.1f%%   (consumer too slow)" \
          [expr {100.0*$st_slv/$total}]]
    puts [format "starvation        %.1f%%   (producer too slow)" \
          [expr {100.0*$st_mst/$total}]]

    # The verdict, stated rather than left to interpretation:
    if {$st_mst > 2*$st_slv} {
        puts ">> BOTTLENECK IS UPSTREAM. Check outstanding transactions, FIFO"
        puts "   depth and PROG_EMPTY_THRESH. Optimising the consumer is wasted work."
    } elseif {$st_slv > 2*$st_mst} {
        puts ">> BOTTLENECK IS DOWNSTREAM. Check burst length, width conversion,"
        puts "   memory access patterns and arbitration."
    } else {
        puts ">> Balanced. Both sides contribute; look at the clock rate or the"
        puts "   data width before micro-optimising either end."
    }
}

# --- 2. The victory lap: measure at the conditions you will SHIP into ---------
# Timing was signed off at the slow corner. Performance is usually measured at
# room temperature on a bench supply. Close that gap deliberately:
#
#   * Maximum sustained data rate, not a burst
#   * Maximum ambient temperature the enclosure reaches (Section 5.2)
#   * Minimum supply voltage within regulator tolerance (Section 1.2)
#   * The most adversarial data pattern you can construct, not random data
#
# Record all four alongside every number you publish. A throughput figure without
# its conditions is not a measurement, it is an anecdote.
```

### Final Thoughts on the Sprint

System tuning is where you turn a working design into a *great* design. It requires a deep understanding of your data flow and a mastery of your monitoring tools. By identifying bottlenecks and balancing throughput against latency, you ensure that your FPGA performs at its absolute peak. You’ve followed the UltraFast Design Methodology from start to finish. Now, take your victory lap. You’ve earned it.

### Answers

**Brain Power** — *HFT: throughput or latency? Why would an extra register on a 400 MHz
path hurt, even though it makes timing easier?*

**Latency, overwhelmingly** — and specifically *tail* latency, the worst case rather than
the average. In high-frequency trading the economic value of a response decays to zero
within the time it takes a competitor to respond. Throughput matters only insofar as you
must not drop messages; being able to process ten million messages per second is worth
nothing if your response to the one that mattered arrived 40 ns late.

Why an extra register hurts, concretely. One pipeline stage at 400 MHz is:

$$\frac{1}{400\times10^{6}} = 2.5\ \text{ns}$$

For a tick-to-trade path with a total budget of, say, 200 ns, that is **1.25 % of the
entire budget for one register.** A pipelining exercise that adds eight stages across a
datapath costs 20 ns — 10 % of the budget — in exchange for a frequency increase that buys
throughput nobody is paying for.

The subtler point, and the one that makes this a good question: **making timing easier is
not free even when it works.** The engineer who pipelines to close timing has optimised
the metric the tool reports (WNS) rather than the metric the customer buys (latency). It is
the same failure as optimising the component that was already fast enough.

What a latency-optimised design does instead: run at a lower clock frequency with wider,
shallower logic (fewer cycles, each longer), use combinational paths where they close, keep
the datapath physically short with floorplanning, and cut through rather than store —
process a packet header as it arrives rather than buffering the frame. All of these are the
*opposite* of what a throughput-optimised design does, which is why "make it faster" is an
ambiguous instruction until someone says which axis.

**Sharpen Your Pencil #1** — 220-cycle round trip, 1 beat/cycle consumption, 32-beat
bursts.

FIFO depth, from Little's Law:

$$L = \lambda W = 1 \times 220 = 220\ \text{beats minimum}$$

Round up to **256** (power of two, plus 36 beats of margin for latency variation — real
memory latency is not constant, and a FIFO sized to the *average* stalls on every
above-average round trip).

Outstanding transactions:

$$N \ge \frac{\text{round-trip latency}}{\text{burst duration}} = \frac{220}{32} = 6.875 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad 7$$

So: **256-deep FIFO and at least 7 outstanding transactions.** Both are required — they are
not alternatives. The FIFO absorbs the latency for data already requested; the outstanding
transaction depth is what keeps requests continuously in flight so the FIFO gets refilled
before it drains. A design with a 256-deep FIFO and 2 outstanding transactions achieves

$$\frac{2 \times 32}{220} = 29\ \%$$

of line rate, with a mostly-empty FIFO, and the FIFO will look like the innocent party
throughout.

**Sharpen Your Pencil #2** — 500,000 cycles, 180,000 beats, 240,000 starved, 45,000
backpressured.

$$\text{Efficiency} = \frac{180{,}000}{500{,}000} = 36\ \%$$

(The remaining 35,000 cycles are fully idle — neither side wanted anything.)

Diagnosis in one sentence: **the consumer is waiting on the producer 48 % of the time
against 9 % the other way, so the bottleneck is upstream and the fix is more data in
flight — outstanding transactions, FIFO depth, and prefetch threshold — not anything on the
consumer side.**

**Sharpen Your Pencil #3** — 5 stages at 200 MHz versus 9 stages at 350 MHz.

$$\text{Latency}_{200} = \frac{5}{200\times10^{6}} = 25.0\ \text{ns}$$

$$\text{Latency}_{350} = \frac{9}{350\times10^{6}} = 25.7\ \text{ns}$$

$$\text{Throughput change} = \frac{350}{200} = 1.75\times$$

So: **75 % more throughput for 0.7 ns (2.8 %) more latency.**

- **Video scaler: yes, clearly.** Throughput is the entire requirement — pixels per second
  determines the resolution and frame rate you can support. An extra 0.7 ns is
  imperceptible against a frame time of 16.7 ms, and the 1.75× may be the difference
  between supporting 4K60 and not.
- **Motor control loop: it depends, and probably not for the reason you expect.** The
  latency increase is trivial in absolute terms; a control loop running at, say, 20 kHz has
  a 50 µs period, and 0.7 ns is nothing. So the honest answer is that *this particular
  trade* is harmless for the motor controller too — but it is also **pointless**, because
  the extra throughput has no use. You have added four pipeline stages, four registers'
  worth of area, and four stages of additional verification surface, to gain a capability
  the system will never exercise.

The general lesson, and it is the one worth keeping: **do not optimise a metric the system
does not buy.** For the scaler, throughput is the product. For the motor controller, the
correct question is not "can I make it faster" but "what is my worst-case loop delay, and
does it have margin" — and the answer to that comes from the *maximum* latency under all
conditions, not from the clock rate.

### There Are No Dumb Questions

**Q: My design meets timing at 400 MHz. Why is my throughput not 400 million beats per
second?**

A: Because timing closure guarantees your clock, not your data flow. Between the two sit
stalls: an interface with nothing to send, a FIFO that emptied, an arbiter that granted
someone else, a burst that ended and whose successor has not been requested. Efficiency —
beats divided by cycles — is a separate measurement from $F_{max}$, and it is usually the
one that is wrong. That is what the stall counters are for.

**Q: Where should I put the performance counters?**

A: On every interface where a producer meets a consumer, and permanently. They cost a few
hundred flops each, which is nothing, and they convert a field report of "the system feels
slow" into a stall ratio you can act on without reproducing anything. Instrumentation you
add during a performance investigation always arrives after you needed it.

**Q: Should I use the AXI Performance Monitor IP or roll my own counters?**

A: The APM gives you far more — latency histograms, per-ID tracking, address profiling —
and it is the right tool for a serious investigation of a complex interconnect. Simple
stall counters give you the *diagnosis* (which side is waiting) for a fraction of the
resources, and can stay in the production design. Use counters everywhere by default, and
bring in the APM when the counters tell you which interface deserves the scrutiny.

**Q: Is bigger always better for FIFO depth?**

A: No, and there are two costs. BRAM is finite, and a FIFO sized far beyond the
latency-bandwidth product consumes memory a real feature could have used. More importantly,
**a deep FIFO adds latency when it is full** — in a latency-sensitive system a 4096-deep
buffer is a way to make your worst-case response time terrible while your average looks
excellent. Size to the calculation plus margin, not to whatever fits.

**Q: How do I find the bottleneck when there are twenty interfaces?**

A: Do not stare at twenty. Start at the *output* and walk backwards: the last stage is
either producing at line rate or it is not; if it is starved, look at what feeds it; repeat.
The bottleneck is the first stage you reach that is *not* starved, because everything
downstream of it is waiting on it. This converges in a few steps regardless of how many
interfaces exist, and it stops you optimising a stage that was never the constraint.

**Q: My throughput measurement varies between runs. Which number do I publish?**

A: The worst one, with its conditions attached. And investigate the variance — if
throughput varies run to run on identical stimulus, something is non-deterministic
(arbitration, memory refresh timing, an asynchronous crossing) and the *variance itself*
may be the finding. A system whose throughput ranges from 80 % to 95 % of line rate has a
15 % problem hiding in it.

### Bullet Points

- **Little's Law**: $L = \lambda W$. Items in flight = rate × latency. It sizes every
  buffer, every prefetch distance, and every outstanding-transaction count you will build.
- **FIFO depth** ≥ round-trip latency × consumption rate. Add margin for latency
  *variation*, then round up to a power of two.
- **Outstanding transactions** ≥ round-trip latency ÷ burst duration. FIFO depth and
  outstanding depth are both required; neither substitutes for the other.
- **`PROG_EMPTY_THRESH`, not empty.** Requesting the next burst at empty guarantees a stall
  of one full round trip.
- **Diagnose with the stall ratio.** `!VALID && READY` = starvation (bottleneck upstream);
  `VALID && !READY` = backpressure (bottleneck downstream). Measure before optimising
  either.
- **Efficiency = beats ÷ cycles**, and it is a different number from $F_{max}$. Meeting
  timing at 400 MHz does not deliver 400 M beats/s.
- **Throughput and latency trade against each other.** A pipeline stage costs
  $1/f_{\text{clk}}$ of latency. Know which axis your product is sold on before optimising.
- **Walk backwards from the output** to find a bottleneck among many interfaces.
- **Publish measurements with their conditions**: sustained rate, worst-case temperature,
  minimum voltage, adversarial data. A number without conditions is an anecdote.
- **Commands and parameters**: `xpm_fifo_sync` / `xpm_fifo_async` with `READ_MODE("fwft")`
  and `PROG_EMPTY_THRESH`; `refresh_hw_device` and `get_hw_probe_value` to read counters
  from the hardware manager; `report_utilization` to price the buffering you just sized.
