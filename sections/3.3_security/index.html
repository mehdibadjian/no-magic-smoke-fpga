    Section 3.3: Configuration and Security: Locking the Vault

    An FPGA is fundamentally a blank slate. When you turn off the power, it forgets everything it ever knew. Every time you turn it back on, it has to load its personality from an external memory source—a process we call Configuration. If you don't understand how your FPGA "wakes up," you’re building a brain without a nervous system.

    But it’s not just about getting the bitstream into the chip. In today’s world, your design is your most valuable asset. If a competitor can just sniff the configuration wires and steal your bitstream, they’ve stolen your entire product. Configuration and Security are the two sides of the same coin: you need the FPGA to wake up reliably, and you need it to wake up securely.

    The Wake-Up Call: The Configuration Sequence

    When power is applied to an AMD FPGA, it goes through a very specific ritual. First, it clears its internal memory (Housecleaning). Then, it samples its Mode Pins to see where it should look for its bitstream (The Search). Finally, it starts pulling in data, verifying it, and—if everything is correct—it asserts the DONE pin and starts running (The Activation).

    If the DONE pin never goes high, your FPGA is essentially a very expensive space heater. Most configuration failures are caused by signal integrity issues on the flash memory interface or incorrect mode pin settings. The UltraFast Methodology emphasizes checking your configuration timing and signal quality as early as possible in the board bring-up phase.

    Brain Power If your configuration flash memory is too slow, will the FPGA wait for it? Or will it time out and fail to boot? How do you coordinate the power-on-reset (POR) of the FPGA with the readiness of the flash chip?

    Choosing Your Interface: SPI, BPI, or JTAG?

    There are several ways to get a bitstream into an FPGA. JTAG is the slow, reliable interface used for debugging. SPI (Serial Peripheral Interface) is the most common for production, as it uses small, cheap flash chips. BPI (Byte Peripheral Interface) is faster but uses more pins.

    The choice depends on your requirements. If you need your FPGA to be ready to process data within 100 milliseconds of power-on (like a PCIe device), you might need a high-speed BPI interface or a quad-SPI setup. If speed doesn't matter, a standard SPI chip is usually the way to go. Pick the right "keyhole" for your design's needs.

    Fireside Chat: The JTAG Cable and the SPI Flash

    JTAG Cable: "I'm the doctor. I can see inside your brain, I can step through your logic, and I can load a bitstream even if your board is half-broken. I'm the ultimate debugger."

    SPI Flash: "That’s nice, but you're a cable. When the product is in the customer's hands, you're back in the lab. I'm the one who lives on the board. I'm the one who wakes the FPGA up every single morning."

    JTAG Cable: "Yeah, but you're slow. It takes you forever to load a 100MB bitstream. I can do a 'boundary scan' and tell the engineer exactly which pin is shorted to ground."

    SPI Flash: "Speed doesn't matter if you aren't there. I'm the permanent memory. Just make sure the engineer doesn't mess up my clock frequency, or I'll stay silent and the whole system will be a brick."

    The Safety Net: Multi-Boot and the Golden Image

    Imagine you’re performing a remote firmware update on a system in a satellite or a deep-sea sensor. If the power fails during the update, the bitstream in the flash becomes corrupted. Your FPGA will never boot again. You’ve just created a multi-million dollar piece of e-waste.

    To prevent this, we use Multi-Boot. We store a "Golden Image" (a known-good, minimal version of the design) at the beginning of the flash memory. We store the "Update Image" further down. If the update image fails its checksum, the FPGA automatically falls back to the Golden Image. It’s a safety net that saves you from a world of hurt.

    Bitstream Encryption: Protecting the Crown Jewels

    If someone gets a copy of your bitstream, they can reverse-engineer your logic or, worse, clone your entire product. AMD FPGAs support AES-256 encryption. You can load a secret key into the FPGA’s internal battery-backed RAM (BBRAM) or eFUSEs.

    Once encrypted, the bitstream is just a pile of random numbers to anyone who doesn't have the key. The FPGA decrypts the bitstream internally as it loads. This is the gold standard for IP protection. If you’re building anything remotely proprietary, you must encrypt your bitstream.

    Authentication and Secure Boot: Trust but Verify

    Encryption prevents people from reading your code. Authentication prevents people from replacing your code. By using digital signatures (RSA or ECDSA), the FPGA can verify that the bitstream it’s loading was actually created by you and hasn't been tampered with.

    This is the core of "Secure Boot." It ensures that no "malicious" bitstream can ever run on your hardware. In a world where hardware-level hacking is becoming more common, authentication is just as important as encryption. Trust your bitstream, but verify it every time you boot.

    Code Detective: The Multi-Boot Tcl Script

    You can use Tcl commands in Vivado to set the jump addresses for your Multi-Boot fallback.

    # Code Detective: Why will this fallback fail?
set_property BITSTREAM.CONFIG.CONFIGFALLBACK ENABLE [current_design]
set_property BITSTREAM.CONFIG.NEXT_CONFIG_REBOOT_ADDR 0x01000000 [current_design]
# Hint: What happens if the Golden Image itself is corrupted?

    The flaw is that the two properties describe the update image's jump, and nothing here establishes the golden image the device falls back to. Enabling CONFIGFALLBACK without a valid, protected image at the fallback address gives you a device that detects the corruption, jumps, and fails again — an infinite reboot loop rather than a recovery.

    There is a second, subtler flaw: no watchdog. Fallback triggers on a detected failure — a bad CRC, an ID mismatch. It does not trigger when the bitstream loads perfectly and the design is broken, which is the more common outcome of a bad update.

    The fix. Two images, two sets of properties, and a timer:

    # --- GOLDEN image, written to flash address 0x00000000 --------------------------
# Built once, verified exhaustively, and never updated in the field. Its only jobs
# are: bring up the configuration interface, and be able to reprogram the update
# image. Keep it small and boring.
set_property BITSTREAM.CONFIG.CONFIGFALLBACK        ENABLE     [current_design]
set_property BITSTREAM.CONFIG.NEXT_CONFIG_ADDR      0x01000000 [current_design]
# ^ "if I am healthy, jump to the update image at 0x01000000"
set_property BITSTREAM.CONFIG.SPI_BUSWIDTH          4          [current_design]
set_property BITSTREAM.CONFIG.CONFIGRATE            51.0       [current_design]

# --- UPDATE image, written to flash address 0x01000000 --------------------------
# The field-updatable design. Note it does NOT enable a further jump: it is the
# destination, and its failure path is back to golden.
set_property BITSTREAM.CONFIG.CONFIGFALLBACK        ENABLE     [current_design]
set_property BITSTREAM.CONFIG.NEXT_CONFIG_ADDR      0x00000000 [current_design]

# --- The watchdog that catches "loaded fine, design is broken" ------------------
# TIMER_CFG arms a configuration watchdog. If the design does not clear it in time,
# the device reconfigures from the fallback address. Without this, a bitstream that
# passes CRC and then hangs leaves you with a brick.
set_property BITSTREAM.CONFIG.TIMER_CFG             0x1FFFFFF  [current_design]

    The resulting flash map, which belongs in your design documentation because the person doing the field update will need it:

    Flash address Contents Updatable in field?
    0x00000000 Golden image No — write-protect this region
    0x01000000 Update image Yes
    0x02000000 Application data / second update slot Yes

    And the rule that makes it all work: write-protect the golden region in the flash device itself. A fallback scheme whose golden image can be erased by the same update path that erased the application is not a fallback scheme.

    Analogy → Silicon

    In the story In the silicon Where the analogy breaks
    Waking up and loading a personality The configuration sequence: clear, sample mode pins M[2:0], load, CRC-check, assert DONE Waking is gradual; configuration is a state machine with hard failure points, and DONE staying low tells you nothing about which one you hit
    Picking the right keyhole The configuration interface: master SPI (×1/×2/×4/×8), BPI, SelectMAP, JTAG A keyhole either fits or not; interface choice is a bandwidth decision measured in milliseconds of boot time, computed below
    A safety net Multi-boot with CONFIGFALLBACK + NEXT_CONFIG_ADDR + TIMER_CFG A net catches everything; fallback catches detected failures — CRC and ID — and catches "loads fine, hangs" only if you armed the watchdog
    Golden image A minimal, exhaustively verified bitstream at address 0x00000000 in a write-protected flash region Gold is valuable; this image's value is entirely in being boring and unchanging, and any pressure to add features to it is a bug
    Locking the vault AES-GCM 256 bitstream encryption, key in BBRAM (battery-backed) or eFUSE (permanent) A vault can be reopened; eFUSE is a one-way operation, and a wrong key burned into eFUSE is a scrapped board
    Verifying the signature RSA-based authentication / secure boot A signature check is a yes/no; secure boot is a chain, and it is only as strong as the root of trust you burned into the device

    Do the Math

    Configuration time is a real system requirement — a PCIe endpoint must respond within a specified window after power-good, and "the FPGA is still loading" is not an acceptable answer. The calculation is straightforward and almost nobody does it before choosing a flash device.

    \[T_{\text{config}} = \frac{S_{\text{bitstream}}\ [\text{bits}]}{W_{\text{bus}} \times f_{\text{CONFIGRATE}}}\]

    Givens — get the bitstream size from your own write_bitstream log, and the maximum CONFIGRATE from the device datasheet and the flash device's datasheet, whichever is lower:

    Symbol Meaning Value
    \(S\) Uncompressed bitstream 128 Mb
    \(W\) SPI bus width ×1, then ×4
    \(f\) CONFIGRATE 12.5 MHz, then 51.0 MHz

    Case A — the default nobody changes: ×1 SPI at 12.5 MHz.

    \[T = \frac{128\times10^{6}\ \text{bits}}{1 \times 12.5\times10^{6}\ \text{bit/s}} = 10.24\ \text{s}\]

    Over ten seconds. A PCIe host will have finished enumeration and given up on you long before that.

    Case B — quad SPI at 51 MHz, which costs three extra board traces and two lines of Tcl:

    \[T = \frac{128\times10^{6}}{4 \times 51\times10^{6}} = 0.627\ \text{s}\]

    627 ms — a 16× improvement, from BITSTREAM.CONFIG.SPI_BUSWIDTH 4 and BITSTREAM.CONFIG.CONFIGRATE 51.0, plus making sure the board actually routes all four data lines to the flash.

    Case C — add compression, which costs nothing but a property:

    set_property BITSTREAM.GENERAL.COMPRESS TRUE [current_design]

    Compression ratio depends entirely on how much of the device is unused (it works by collapsing repeated configuration frames). A design filling 60 % of the device might compress to 70 Mb:

    \[T = \frac{70\times10^{6}}{4 \times 51\times10^{6}} = 0.343\ \text{s}\]

    Verdict: 10.24 s → 0.34 s, a 30× improvement, entirely from properties and one board decision made early. And note the direction of the trade — compression makes configuration faster but is incompatible with per-frame readback verification in some flows, and encryption changes the size, so recompute after you enable either.

    One more number worth having: encryption does not slow configuration measurably on UltraScale+ — the AES engine runs at the configuration data rate. The reason people skip encryption is inconvenience of key management, not boot time, and that is worth saying out loud when someone proposes shipping unencrypted.

    The Real Artifact

    A complete, ordered bitstream property set for a production design with encryption, authentication and fallback — plus the flash programming commands. This is a file you edit and keep, not a snippet.

    #==============================================================================
# bitstream.xdc / bitstream.tcl - production configuration settings.
#
# ORDER OF OPERATIONS MATTERS FOR THE KEYS:
#   1. Decide BBRAM vs eFUSE before you build anything. eFUSE is PERMANENT.
#   2. Generate the key OUTSIDE this repo. Never commit a .nky file.
#   3. Program the key into the device, THEN load an encrypted bitstream.
#      An encrypted bitstream loaded into a device with no key fails silently
#      (DONE stays low) and looks exactly like a board fault.
#==============================================================================

#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 1. INTERFACE AND SPEED - see "Do the Math" for why these two lines matter
#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
set_property BITSTREAM.CONFIG.SPI_BUSWIDTH   4        [current_design]
set_property BITSTREAM.CONFIG.CONFIGRATE     51.0     [current_design]
set_property BITSTREAM.GENERAL.COMPRESS      TRUE     [current_design]

# Unused pins: pull them to a defined state rather than leaving them floating.
set_property BITSTREAM.CONFIG.UNUSEDPIN      PULLDOWN [current_design]

# CFGBVS/CONFIG_VOLTAGE must match your board. Omitting them fires DRC CFGBVS-1 at
# write_bitstream, and it is the single most common first-bitstream failure.
set_property CFGBVS      VCCO [current_design]
set_property CONFIG_VOLTAGE 3.3 [current_design]

# Check before you spend an hour on write_bitstream:
#   report_drc -checks {CFGBVS-1 UCIO-1 NSTD-1}

#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 2. MULTI-BOOT - the flash map from the Code Detective section above
#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
set_property BITSTREAM.CONFIG.CONFIGFALLBACK   ENABLE     [current_design]
set_property BITSTREAM.CONFIG.NEXT_CONFIG_ADDR 0x01000000 [current_design]
set_property BITSTREAM.CONFIG.TIMER_CFG        0x1FFFFFF  [current_design]

#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. ENCRYPTION - confidentiality. Stops cloning and reverse engineering.
#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
set_property BITSTREAM.ENCRYPTION.ENCRYPT          YES     [current_design]
set_property BITSTREAM.ENCRYPTION.ENCRYPTKEYSELECT BBRAM   [current_design]
#                                                  ^ or EFUSE - decide once, forever
set_property BITSTREAM.ENCRYPTION.KEYFILE  "../keys/design.nky" [current_design]

#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 4. AUTHENTICATION - integrity and origin. Stops substitution.
#    Encryption alone does NOT stop an attacker replacing your bitstream with
#    their own; it only stops them reading yours. You need both.
#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
set_property BITSTREAM.RSA.AUTHENTICATE       YES [current_design]
set_property BITSTREAM.RSA.RSAPRIVATEKEYFILE  "../keys/rsa_private.pem" [current_design]

#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 5. LOCK IT DOWN - once the keys are in, close the doors that bypass them
#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Disable the JTAG path that could load an unencrypted bitstream over the top.
# Do this LAST, and be certain: on a device with eFUSE keys this is not reversible,
# and you have just removed your own debug access.
# set_property BITSTREAM.GENERAL.JTAG_SYSMON  DISABLE [current_design]

write_bitstream -force ./build/top.bit

#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 6. FLASH IMAGE - combine golden + update into one programming file
#------------------------------------------------------------------------------
write_cfgmem -force -format MCS -interface SPIx4 -size 128 \
    -loadbit "up 0x00000000 ./build/golden.bit up 0x01000000 ./build/top.bit" \
    ./build/flash_image.mcs

# Verify what you built, before it reaches a board:
#   - Check the .bit header says the part and the encryption state you expect
#   - Confirm the .mcs record addresses match the table in your documentation
#   - Program a bench board and pull power mid-write to exercise the fallback.
#     A fallback path that has never been tested is a fallback path that does not work.

    Sharpen Your Pencil

    1. Your bitstream is 220 Mb and your system spec requires configuration to complete within 500 ms. Compute the required \(W \times f\) product, then say whether ×4 SPI at 51 MHz is sufficient, and what your options are if it is not.
    2. A colleague proposes encrypting the bitstream but not authenticating it, arguing that "if they can't read it, they can't change it." Describe an attack that defeats this.
    3. Your golden image at 0x00000000 boots correctly, and the update image at 0x01000000 loads and then hangs with DONE high. Which of the three multi-boot properties saves you, and what does the recovery sequence look like?

    Final Thoughts on the Vault

    Configuration is the bridge between the physical board and the digital logic. Security is the wall that protects that logic from the outside world. By mastering Multi-Boot, encryption, and authentication, you ensure that your design is not only performant but also resilient and secure. Lock the vault, keep the keys safe, and let your FPGA wake up with confidence.

    Answers

    Brain Power — If the configuration flash is too slow, does the FPGA wait, or time out? How do you coordinate POR with flash readiness?

    In master modes, the FPGA drives the clock — so it does not "wait" in the sense of adapting. It clocks data out of the flash at whatever CONFIGRATE you set. If the flash cannot deliver at that rate, you do not get a graceful slowdown; you get wrong data, which fails the CRC, which aborts configuration and leaves DONE low. The symptom is a board that works at 12.5 MHz and fails at 51 MHz, or worse, works at room temperature and fails cold.

    So the coordination has two parts, and they are different problems:

    1. Rate compatibility. CONFIGRATE must be within the flash's specification at your worst-case temperature and voltage, including the read-access time of the specific command mode you are using (quad read with the right number of dummy cycles). This is a datasheet cross-check, done once, at design time.
    2. Readiness at power-on. The flash needs its own power-up time before it will respond. The FPGA begins configuration when INIT_B releases after POR. If the flash is not ready, the first reads return garbage. The fix is to hold PROGRAM_B low until the flash rail is stable and its power-up time has elapsed — usually from the same sequencer or supervisor that handles the rest of the power sequencing (Section 1.2).

    The general principle: configuration is a system behaviour with a power sequencing dependency, not an FPGA-only behaviour. It belongs on the same timing diagram as your rails.

    Sharpen Your Pencil #1 — 220 Mb in 500 ms:

    \[W \times f \ge \frac{S}{T} = \frac{220\times10^{6}\ \text{bits}}{0.5\ \text{s}} = 440\times10^{6}\ \text{bit/s}\]

    ×4 SPI at 51 MHz gives:

    \[4 \times 51\times10^{6} = 204\times10^{6}\ \text{bit/s}\]

    Not sufficient — it delivers 204 Mb/s against a requirement of 440 Mb/s, so configuration would take

    \[\frac{220\times10^{6}}{204\times10^{6}} = 1.08\ \text{s}\]

    more than twice the budget. Options, roughly in order of cost:

    1. Compression. Free, and often the whole answer: if the design compresses to under 102 Mb you meet the budget with no hardware change. Check your actual ratio — it depends on device utilisation, so measure rather than assume.
    2. Faster CONFIGRATE. Check what the device and the flash both support; some combinations reach higher rates. This costs nothing if the flash can take it.
    3. ×8 SPI or dual quad-SPI. Doubles the rate to 408 Mb/s — still marginally short, but with compression comfortably clear. Costs board pins and layout.
    4. BPI (parallel) flash. Much higher bandwidth, at the cost of a lot of pins.
    5. SelectMAP driven by an external controller (a microcontroller or another FPGA). Highest bandwidth, most system complexity, and it is what high-end PCIe cards generally do.

    Note that option 1 is free and options 3–5 are board respins. This is the argument for doing this arithmetic during planning, which is why this section sits in the design creation chapter and not in bring-up.

    Sharpen Your Pencil #2 — The attack that defeats encryption-without-authentication:

    Substitution. The attacker does not need to read your bitstream. They write their own bitstream — an unencrypted one — into the flash, replacing yours. On power-up the device loads it. Unless the device is configured to require an authenticated (and encrypted) image, it will happily accept a plaintext one.

    What they gain: complete control of your hardware. They can add a backdoor to a design they never understood, exfiltrate data from your board's interfaces, or simply use your product as a platform. They never learned a single thing about your IP, which was the only thing encryption protected.

    A second attack in the same family: rollback. If an attacker can capture an older, legitimately encrypted bitstream of yours — one with a known vulnerability you have since fixed — they can write it back. Encryption does not help because the image is genuinely yours. Authentication with version enforcement is what stops this.

    The one-line version for the design review: encryption protects confidentiality; authentication protects integrity. They are orthogonal, and shipping only one leaves the other property entirely undefended.

    Sharpen Your Pencil #3 — The update image loads (CRC passes) and then hangs with DONE high.

    • CONFIGFALLBACK does not save you. It triggers on a configuration failure — bad CRC, ID mismatch — and there was none. The bitstream loaded perfectly.
    • NEXT_CONFIG_ADDR does not save you either; it is the jump target, and the jump already happened.
    • TIMER_CFG is what saves you. It arms a watchdog during configuration; if the design does not clear the timer within the configured window, the device treats it as a failure and reconfigures from the fallback address — back to golden.

    The recovery sequence, end to end:

    1. Power on. Golden image at 0x00000000 loads, sees NEXT_CONFIG_ADDR = 0x01000000, and triggers a warm boot to the update image.
    2. Update image loads, CRC passes, DONE goes high, watchdog armed by TIMER_CFG starts counting.
    3. The design is broken and never clears the watchdog.
    4. Timer expires. The device reconfigures from 0x00000000.
    5. Golden image is running. It must now not immediately jump to the update image again, or you have built a reboot loop — so golden needs a way to know it arrived via fallback. The BOOTSTS register records the reason for the last configuration; read it and, on a fallback indication, stay in golden and wait for a new update.

    That last step is the one people leave out, and it converts a working recovery mechanism into an infinite loop. Test it by deliberately shipping a broken update image to a bench board. A fallback path you have never exercised is a fallback path that does not work.

    There Are No Dumb Questions

    Q: BBRAM or eFUSE for the AES key?

    A: BBRAM is battery-backed RAM: the key is erasable and replaceable, and it is lost if the battery dies — which is both its security feature (tamper response can zeroise it) and its logistics problem (a coin cell with a service life). eFUSE is permanent: no battery, no maintenance, and no second chances. Choose eFUSE for high-volume products where key rotation is not part of the plan, BBRAM where you need to re-key or where a tamper-detection circuit should be able to destroy the key. Decide before the first production run, because it affects the board.

    Q: DONE never goes high. Where do I start?

    A: In this order, because it goes from most to least common: (1) INIT_B — if it is being pulled low, configuration was aborted, usually by a CRC error; (2) mode pins M[2:0] — are they strapped for the interface you actually built for; (3) the flash — is it powered, does its CS toggle, is there activity on the clock; (4) CFGBVS/CONFIG_VOLTAGE — do they match the board's bank voltage; (5) encryption state — an encrypted bitstream into a device without the key fails exactly like a hardware fault. A scope on INIT_B, DONE and the flash CS answers most of these in five minutes.

    Q: How large is my bitstream, and where do I find out?

    A: The write_bitstream log reports it, and the .bit file size on disk is a close proxy. It is a property of the device, not of your design — an empty design and a full design produce nearly the same size uncompressed, because the bitstream configures every frame in the device. That is why compression helps proportionally to how empty the device is.

    Q: Do I need any of this for a prototype?

    A: You need CFGBVS/CONFIG_VOLTAGE correct (it is a DRC error otherwise) and you need the interface and rate to match your board. You can defer encryption, authentication and multi-boot. But make the decision early, because the golden image needs flash space reserved for it and re-partitioning flash after the fact is a board and firmware change.

    Q: Is bitstream encryption actually unbreakable?

    A: It is AES-256, so the cipher is not the weak point — key handling is. Keys committed to a repository, keys shared across a product line, keys logged by a programming station, and unprotected JTAG paths that allow loading unencrypted images over the top all defeat it without touching the cryptography. Treat the .nky file with the same discipline you would a production signing key, and never let it near version control.

    Bullet Points

    • Configuration time \(= S_{\text{bits}} / (W_{\text{bus}} \times f_{\text{CONFIGRATE}})\). Compute it before choosing a flash device.
    • ×1 at 12.5 MHz → 10.24 s for a 128 Mb bitstream. ×4 at 51 MHz with compression → 0.34 s. Two properties and one board decision.
    • Properties that matter: SPI_BUSWIDTH, CONFIGRATE, GENERAL.COMPRESS, UNUSEDPIN, CFGBVS, CONFIG_VOLTAGE.
    • Multi-boot needs three things: CONFIGFALLBACK ENABLE, NEXT_CONFIG_ADDR, and TIMER_CFG. Without the timer you catch only detected failures, not "loads fine, hangs".
    • Write-protect the golden region in the flash device. Otherwise the update path can erase its own recovery.
    • Read BOOTSTS in the golden image so a fallback boot does not immediately jump back into the broken update image.
    • Encryption ≠ authentication. Encryption stops reading and cloning; authentication stops substitution and rollback. Ship both.
    • BBRAM is re-keyable and needs a battery; eFUSE is permanent. Decide before the first production run.
    • Never commit a .nky or a private key. The cipher is not the weak point; key handling is.
    • Test the fallback path deliberately by shipping a broken update to a bench board.
    • Commands: write_bitstream, write_cfgmem -format MCS -interface SPIx4, report_drc -checks {CFGBVS-1 UCIO-1 NSTD-1}, and program_hw_devices for the bench.
